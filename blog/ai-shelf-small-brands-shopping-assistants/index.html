AI Shopping Assistants: How Small Brands Get Recommended on the AI Shelf

Shoppers now ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity what to buy. Here's how small brands earn a place on the AI shelf with clear product facts, consistent listings and a monthly audit.

The question behind this article

How can I use AI to improve my marketing and get my products recommended by AI shopping assistants?

Short answerSmall brands get recommended by AI shopping assistants by making products easy to find, understand and verify: answer-style product pages, structured data, identical facts across every marketplace, honest comparison content and mentions on other sites. Then check monthly what ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google's AI Mode say. Adobe expects AI-assisted holiday shopping traffic to rise 130%.

Key takeaways

  • The AI shelf is the short list of products an AI assistant names when a shopper asks what to buy; if you're not in the answer, you're not on the shelf.
  • Adobe forecasts that AI-assisted shopping traffic to US retail sites will rise 130% year over year this holiday season, far faster than paid search or social referrals.
  • Modern Retail reports that a writer got AI bots to recommend a completely fictional brand, which shows assistants respond to findable information, not just big budgets.
  • Clear product facts, structured data, consistent listings and mentions on other sites are the inputs a small brand controls.
  • Measure progress with your AI-citation share on a fixed set of buying questions and with AI referral traffic in your analytics.

How can a small brand use AI to market better? The most useful answer this holiday season isn't writing captions faster. It's making sure that when a shopper asks ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or Google's AI Mode what to buy, your product is one of the answers. That comes down to publishing clear, consistent, checkable facts about what you sell, everywhere an AI assistant might look.

Retail now has a name for this: the AI shelf. Modern Retail describes it as the newest layer of product discovery, after physical shelves and online storefronts, as consumers ask assistants like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini for recommendations. The traffic is real too. Adobe forecasts that AI-assisted shopping traffic to US retail sites will rise 130% year over year this holiday season, Bloomberg reported, while overall online spending grows at a single-digit rate.

What is the AI shelf?

Think of the shelf at a grocery store. For decades, the brands at eye level were the ones that could pay for placement and supply the volume. Search engines opened things up, but the top of the results page still tended to go to whoever had the biggest ad budget and the most backlinks.

On the AI shelf, a shopper asks a question in plain language, such as "best natural deodorant for sensitive skin," and gets a short list of products with reasons. There's no page two. If you're not in the answer, you're not on the shelf. Working to get into that answer is called GEO, or Generative Engine Optimization: making your brand easy for AI answer engines to find, understand and cite.

Why does it matter so much this holiday season?

Because AI referrals are growing far faster than the channels small brands already pay for. Adobe's wider forecast puts US holiday online sales at $275.1 billion, up 6.7%, with paid search growing 4% and social referrals 17%. AI-assisted traffic, at 130%, is in a different league.

Those visits also arrive with context. A shopper who asked an assistant to compare three options and then clicked through to your store did the research before landing. Your product page mainly has to confirm what the assistant already said.

Can a small brand really compete with big ones on the AI shelf?

It can at least get into the conversation. Modern Retail points to an experiment by tech writer Deana Burke, who invented a completely fictional natural deodorant brand to test whether AI bots would recommend it. In the outlet's words, the experiment "actually worked." Modern Retail doesn't detail her method, but the takeaway stands: assistants don't only recommend the brands with the biggest budgets. They recommend what they can find and make sense of.

That matters because of what organizational researchers call resource dependence theory. Businesses depend on resources they don't control, and whoever controls the scarce resource holds the power. For small brands, those resources have been shelf space, ad inventory and specialist expertise, held by retailers, platforms and agencies that cost more than most SMEs can comfortably pay.

The AI shelf changes what's scarce. What an assistant needs is clear, verifiable information, and anyone can publish that. Inequity stifles innovation; shared resources elevate all. Here the shared resource is the open web, and a ten-person brand can document its products as thoroughly as a ten-thousand-person one.

There's a warning in the experiment too. If a brand that doesn't exist can get recommended, accurate and complete information about your real products is your best defense against being out-described by competitors or misdescribed by the assistants themselves.

How do I get my products onto the AI shelf?

Start this week. None of these steps needs a developer for the first pass.

  1. Ask the assistants yourself. Write 15 questions your customers would ask before buying, like "best gift for a home cook under $50." Run them in ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google AI Mode. Note who gets recommended and which websites the answers cite.
  2. Rewrite product pages as answers. State plainly what the product is, who it's for, who it isn't for, key specs, price, sizes, materials or ingredients, shipping and returns. An assistant can't recommend what your page only hints at.
  3. Add structured data. Use schema.org Product markup for price, availability and reviews so machines can read the facts without guessing. Most store platforms offer an app or setting for this.
  4. Make your facts match everywhere. Your site, Amazon listing, Google Merchant Center feed, TikTok Shop and social bios should agree on product names, prices and specs. Conflicting details make your brand harder to describe with confidence.
  5. Publish comparison and buying-guide pages. "Our serum vs. drugstore retinol." "Which size is right for you?" Be honest about trade-offs; balanced pages tend to be more quotable than sales copy.
  6. Earn mentions on other sites. Reviews, niche blogs, gift guides and local press give assistants independent confirmation that you exist and deliver.
  7. Repeat the audit monthly. Rerun the same questions, log what changed and fix the next gap.

How do you know if it's working?

Track two numbers. The first is your AI-citation share: across your test questions, how often your brand is named or your site is cited. The second is AI referral traffic: in your analytics, filter visits referred by sources such as chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai and gemini.google.com, and watch both the trend and the conversion rate.

Frequently asked questions

Is GEO different from SEO?

Yes, but they overlap. SEO (search engine optimization) aims to rank your pages in search results. GEO, generative engine optimization, aims to get your brand named or cited inside AI-generated answers. GEO builds on SEO basics like crawlable pages and helpful content, then adds answer-style writing, structured data, consistent facts across sites and independent mentions. Keep doing SEO; GEO extends it.

Do I need to sell on Amazon to show up in AI shopping answers?

Not necessarily. AI assistants draw on many kinds of sources, including brand websites, marketplaces, reviews and publications. What matters is that wherever your product appears, the details are accurate and agree with each other. If you do sell on Amazon or TikTok Shop, keep those listings as complete and up to date as your own site.

Which AI assistants should a small brand check?

Start with the ones US shoppers use for product questions: ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, and Gemini. Microsoft Copilot is worth adding once you have a routine. Use the same list of buying questions in each one, because assistants can give different answers and cite different sources for the same question.

How long does it take to appear in AI answers?

There's no fixed timeline. Some assistants search the live web when they answer, so a new or improved page can be picked up fairly quickly; others lean more on what they learned earlier. Make the fixes, rerun the same test questions every month and track the trend rather than expecting an overnight jump.
