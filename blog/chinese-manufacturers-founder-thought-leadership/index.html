Thought Leadership for Manufacturers: Why US Buyers Should See Your Founder

US buyers can't tour your factory from Ohio, but a visible founder can close that gap. Here's how social posts now feed AI answers, which AI tools help, and a 30-day plan.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20268 min readEnglish

The question behind this article

I run marketing for a Chinese manufacturer. Which AI tools should we use, and should our founder really be posting on LinkedIn for US buyers?

Short answerYes. US buyers can't easily verify an overseas factory, and a founder who explains how you actually work closes that gap. Use AI tools to transcribe, translate, draft, subtitle, schedule and score posts, but feed them your founder's real knowledge. Should the founder of a Chinese factory be visible to US buyers, and which AI tools actually help? Short version: yes, and AI should be the founder's translator and editor, not their ghostwriter. A US buyer can't walk your production floor from Ohio. A founder who explains, in plain English, how you hold tolerances, handle defects and turn down bad orders gives that buyer something a spec sheet can't.

Two stories this month make it more urgent. Social Media Today covered an SE Ranking study showing that social platform links appeared in 67.66% of Google AI Overview responses and in 48.58% of non-branded AI Mode queries, compared with 8.4% of ChatGPT answers. A day later, Social Media Today reported that LinkedIn's classifiers now cover every post distributed beyond a user's immediate network, with "94% precision at detecting AI-slop." Social activity feeds AI answers, and generic AI content gets buried. That combination favors real experts.

Why should a factory founder post on social media at all?

Most Chinese manufacturers win business through three doors: trade shows like the Canton Fair, marketplaces like Alibaba.com, and inquiries through their own website. All three put you in a lineup. Your booth sits between dozens of similar booths. Your Alibaba.com profile says "20 years of experience, OEM/ODM, ISO 9001," just like the next one.

A founder's voice is the one thing a competitor can't copy. When the person who built the factory explains why you switched resin suppliers after a failed batch, or what gets checked before every container leaves, buyers hear competence. They also get a name and a face to attach to the inquiry they're about to send.

Buyers do look. Before a first order, procurement teams want to know who they're dealing with. An active LinkedIn profile full of substantive posts answers that question before anyone asks it.

How do social posts end up in AI answers?

Quick definitions first, because the three terms get mixed up:

  • SEO (search engine optimization) gets your pages ranked in Google's regular results so buyers click through.
  • AEO (answer engine optimization) shapes your content so search engines and assistants can lift it as the direct answer, such as a featured snippet or an AI Overview.
  • GEO (generative engine optimization) gets your company mentioned and cited when ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini or Google's AI Mode generate an answer.

The SE Ranking study analyzed 100,000 keywords. It found that YouTube is the most commonly cited source in Google's AI Overviews, and that LinkedIn and Reddit were among the top social platforms ChatGPT referenced. The effect varies a lot by engine, which is why the article concludes "there's no one-size-fits-all approach."

For a manufacturer, the practical read is simple. Your founder's YouTube walkthroughs and LinkedIn posts aren't just social content. They're raw material that Google's AI can draw on when a buyer asks about your category, especially in AI Overviews and AI Mode.

Why buyers can't see your quality, and what fills the gap

Economists call this information asymmetry: one side of a deal knows far more than the other. You know your defect rate, your QC steps and which shortcuts you refuse to take. The buyer in Chicago knows only what you show them. When buyers can't tell good suppliers from bad ones, they protect themselves by assuming average quality and negotiating on price. Good factories end up priced like average ones.

The classic fix is a credible signal, something that's hard to fake. Certifications help, but every serious competitor has them. A founder who shows up week after week, explains real trade-offs and answers hard questions in public is sending a signal that takes time and expertise to produce. That's what makes it believable.

It's also why dashboards alone won't get you there. Analytics can tell you which post got clicks. They can't tell you what a purchasing manager in Texas worries about before a first order. True connection thrives where data fails to reach: in the specific, human answers your founder gives to the questions buyers actually ask.

How to launch founder-led thought leadership in 30 days

  1. Pick three topics only your founder can speak to. For example: how you control quality at volume, a material choice you'd defend to any engineer, and a mistake that changed your process.
  2. Record one 20-minute interview a week. Your marketing lead asks questions in Mandarin, and the founder answers naturally. This is your raw material.
  3. Turn each interview into three or four posts. Use AI to transcribe and draft in US English, keeping the founder's examples and numbers intact.
  4. Have the founder approve every post. If they wouldn't say it to a buyer across a table, cut it.
  5. Publish from the founder's LinkedIn two to three times a week. Add one short YouTube video a month that shows the process you talked about.
  6. Answer every serious comment within a day. Replies are often where a buyer starts a real conversation.
  7. Track the signals that matter. Log inquiries that mention a post or video, profile visits from target companies, and whether Google's AI starts citing your content when you search your category.

What does LinkedIn's AI-slop crackdown mean for manufacturers?

LinkedIn defines AI slop as generic or repetitive posts that add no real insight. According to Social Media Today, more than 1 million people used the platform's new AI-slop report option within about three weeks of launch, and those reports help train its detection systems. Posts that sound like everyone else's will reach fewer people.

For factories with real substance, that's good news. A post that says "We are committed to quality" is slop. A post that explains why you reject a whole batch when three units fail a drop test is not. The difference isn't who typed it. It's whether the post contains something only you know.

Frequently asked questions

Does our founder need to speak fluent English?

No. A founder speaking Mandarin on camera with accurate English subtitles often comes across as more credible than a stiff, scripted English read. What matters is that the written posts are rewritten in natural US English and checked by someone who knows the product. Buyers respond to specifics and candor, not a perfect accent.

Should we post from the company page or the founder's personal profile?

Both, with different jobs. The founder's profile carries opinions, stories and lessons, because people follow people. The company page holds product news, certifications and trade show updates, and reshares the founder's best posts. Keep names, descriptions and links consistent across both so buyers and search engines can connect them.

Is it risky to show our production process publicly?

Share the what and the why, not the recipe. You can explain which tests every batch goes through and why you chose a material without publishing tooling drawings, process settings or customer names. Get written permission before featuring any client, and have a production lead review videos for anything confidential before they go live.

Can AI write all of our thought-leadership posts?

AI can draft them, but it can't supply the experience that makes them worth reading. Posts generated from a one-line prompt tend to sound like everyone else's, which is exactly what LinkedIn now demotes. Use AI to turn your founder's real interviews and notes into polished drafts, then have the founder approve each one.
