Tariffs and marketing strategy: how to rebalance messages and budgets

Tariffs reshuffle entire categories. How to read their impact on consumers, rework messages market by market and move budget without sliding into paralysis.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 7 min read Also available in: English中文Español
The question behind this article

“How are tariffs affecting consumers, and how do I adjust my marketing strategy?”

Short answerTariffs change the structure of your category: costs rise, consumers compare more and local substitutes appear. The answer isn’t to freeze investment but to map exposure by market, listen to how the price conversation is shifting, analyse which messages competitors are moving to and rebalance message and budget country by country, with short tests.

Key takeaways

  • A tariff doesn’t just make a product dearer: it reshuffles several of the category’s five forces at once, from buyer power to the threat of substitutes.
  • Freezing all investment until the picture becomes clearer amounts to planning for a market that no longer exists.
  • According to Campaign, research by Publicis London, System1 and Effie concludes that global campaigns deliver fewer commercial outcomes than work designed for each market.
  • The right message after a cost shock is found by analysing what the whole category is already saying and spotting which angle has been left free.
  • Short tests by market take the risk out of rebalancing: scale what works and cut what doesn’t.

How do tariffs affect a marketing strategy? Far more through what they set off than through the tariff itself: rising costs, prices shifting on the shelf, consumers comparing more, local competitors spotting their chance and budgets frozen “until the picture becomes clearer”. The useful response isn’t to wait. It’s to rebalance message and budget market by market, with a fresh read of what’s happening in the category.

And here comes the classic temptation of every cost crisis: centralise. One global campaign, adapted just enough, to save on production. At the end of September, Campaign published research by Publicis London, System1 and Effie comparing the effectiveness of global campaigns with work designed for each market, and its conclusion makes awkward reading for the cost-cutters: global campaigns deliver fewer commercial outcomes.

When a tariff hits each country differently, that conclusion carries even more weight.

How do tariffs affect consumer behaviour?

There’s no single answer, and you should be wary of anyone who offers one without looking at your category. But some patterns recur, and they’re worth watching:

  • Greater price sensitivity. If the final price goes up, consumers compare more, wait for promotions or put off buying.
  • Shifts in what people value. Durability, guarantees, cost per use or financing start to matter more than novelty.
  • Openness to substitutes. Local brands, own labels or alternative categories get a look-in they didn’t have before.
  • More noise and more mistrust. When everyone is talking about price, the old “quality at the best price” line stops meaning anything.

The key point is that these shifts don’t happen in the same way, or at the same time, in Mexico as in Colombia or Spain. So the reading has to be done market by market, and it has to come from consumers’ real conversation, not from assumptions made in a meeting room.

Why do so many brands stand still when the context changes?

Michael Porter explained it decades ago with his five forces model: a category’s profitability depends on rivalry between competitors, the bargaining power of suppliers and buyers, and the threat of new entrants and substitute products. A tariff doesn’t move just one of those forces. It moves several at once.

Which means the marketing plan signed off last year was designed for a market structure that no longer exists. Keeping it intact isn’t prudence; it’s planning for a ghost market.

The trouble is that stagnation gives no warning. There’s no failed campaign to flag the mistake, just slow erosion: a competitor claiming the durability message first, a local substitute gaining visibility, a consumer getting used to another brand. Stagnation kills opportunities quietly, and by the time it shows up in the sales figures, the territory already has an owner.

How do you rebalance your marketing strategy in the face of tariffs?

A six-step process, designed for teams that sell in several markets:

  1. Map your exposure. Which products depend on affected imports, and in which countries. Not every part of the portfolio suffers equally, and the budget shouldn’t treat it as if it did.
  2. Listen to how consumers talk about price. Before touching the message, check whether the category conversation is shifting towards price, durability, origin or financing, and in which markets.
  3. Analyse the whole category, not just three rivals. Review the ads and organic content of every competitor to see who has changed message and which angle has been left free.
  4. Rewrite the message by market. Value, cost per use, guarantee, service or local presence: the argument that works in one country can sound out of place in another.
  5. Redistribute, don’t freeze. Protect the least exposed markets and products, cut back where margin disappears and ring-fence part of the budget for tests.
  6. Test in short cycles. Two or three message variants per market, over a few weeks, with one clear metric. Scale what works and cut what doesn’t.

Imagine a brand of imported e-bikes selling in Mexico, Colombia and Spain. If costs rise in only one of those markets, the usual global campaign would carry on talking about design in all three. With this process, the message in the affected market shifts to cost per kilometre and the guarantee, while the other two hold their brand territory and make the most of competitors’ distraction.

Global campaign or local messages in uncertain times?

A global campaign’s efficiency is obvious in the production budget. Its effectiveness, less so. The research Campaign reports points in exactly that direction: what you save by centralising can be lost in commercial outcomes.

That doesn’t mean producing twenty different campaigns. A shared creative platform, with messages, product demonstrations and offers adapted to each market, is usually a sensible balance. What makes no sense is answering a local blow with a global message.

What an agency should bring to its next client meeting

If you work with brands exposed to tariff changes, don’t wait for the client to ask. Turn up with three things: the exposure map by market, a read of how the price conversation has changed and a proposal for message tests over the next six weeks.

That conversation makes the agency a strategic partner precisely when the client needs one most. And it steers clear of the usual alternative: cut, wait and regret it later.

Frequently asked questions

Should we raise prices or absorb the tariff?

It depends on margin, your category’s price sensitivity and what competitors do; it’s a commercial decision, not just a marketing one. What marketing can contribute is evidence: how consumers talk about price, which competitors have already raised prices or launched promotions, and which attributes justify paying more. With that read, a price rise can come with a credible value message.

Should we cut the marketing budget amid tariff uncertainty?

Across-the-board cuts are usually the worst option, because they reduce presence just as the category is reshuffling. It’s wiser to redistribute: protect the least exposed markets and products, cut back where margin disappears and keep part of the budget for quick tests. That way the budget follows the new market structure, not last year’s.

How do I find out which messages my competitors are using?

By systematically reviewing their ads and organic content, not just the ones that turn up in your feed. Competitive intelligence tools can analyse hundreds of ads in a category in minutes and group them by angle: price, durability, origin or guarantee. The aim is to see which territory is saturated and which has been left open for your brand.

Isn’t a global campaign more efficient when budgets are tight?

It’s cheaper to produce, but not necessarily more effective. According to Campaign, the research by Publicis London, System1 and Effie indicates that global campaigns deliver fewer commercial outcomes than local work. When a tariff affects each country differently, adapting the message by market makes even more sense.
