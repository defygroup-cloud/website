Agency pitch strategy: win with a 48-hour category insight sprint

Lead with the client's category, not your credentials. A 48-hour sprint to find the angle no competitor has claimed, built from real ads and posts.

What is the best agency pitch strategy when a brief lands and you have days, not weeks? Lead with the client's category, not your credentials. In 48 hours a small team can map what every competitor is saying in paid and organic content, cluster how the audience talks about the category, and find the angle nobody has claimed. That gives the pitch a point of view the client has not seen, which is the one thing procurement cannot compare on price.

Two stories from 13 October make the case. Digiday reported that Publicis is selling AI transformation directly to chief executives and stepping away from traditional pitches, having won PepsiCo's business without a pitch in September. And in Brazil, Meio & Mensagem reported that the communications agency association Abracom and the advertisers' body ABA have updated their guide to buying communications services, warning that hiring cannot be reduced to an automated comparison of prices and deliverables.

How are agencies winning new business in 2026?

At the top of the market, increasingly without a pitch. Publicis Groupe chairman and CEO Arthur Sadoun said: "We are increasingly seeing CEOs involved in what we do." Digiday reported that the group's Sapient arm was instrumental in winning "the two biggest accounts of the year without a pitch". Third-quarter net revenue rose 5% to €3.529bn, and new clients add a net 2% to organic growth heading into 2027, worth about €300m. Sadoun's line: "The imperative to adapt is immediate. Clients are ready to commit budgets faster."

Not everyone buys the AI story. Digiday quotes Brian Wieser of Madison & Wall: "The only real driver of increased spend is when you get a change in competitive intensity in a category." Keep that sentence. It is the most useful pitch advice in the article.

Further down the market, pitches are not disappearing. They are being reformed. The Abracom and ABA update adds ten practical steps, including detailed briefs, disclosed selection criteria, a stated investment level, protection for agencies' ideas and detailed feedback to every participant. Abracom also recommends payment terms of no more than 60 days. Carina Almeida of Abracom described the work as "predominantly intellectual and relational" services (our translation).

Both stories point the same way. Whether you are walking into a chief executive's office or a formal review, the agency that understands the client's competitive position wins the room.

Why do pitch strategies go stale?

Stagnation is the silent killer of opportunity. In new business it looks like the credentials deck that has not changed in two years, the process slide every agency shows, and case studies chosen because they are recent rather than relevant.

Michael Porter's Five Forces explains why that is fatal. How much value a business can capture, he argued, depends on five pressures: rivalry between competitors, the power of buyers, the power of suppliers, the threat of new entrants and the threat of substitutes. In pitching, three of them are rising at once.

  • Buyer power: procurement teams compare agencies line by line, which is precisely the habit Abracom is pushing back on.
  • Rivalry: more agencies now have similar AI tools, similar research subscriptions and very similar decks.
  • New routes and substitutes: consultancies, tech arms and direct-to-CEO selling take business around the pitch altogether.

When every agency looks the same, the buyer compares on price. The only escape is to bring something the buyer cannot get elsewhere: a sharper read of their own market than they have.

What does a 48-hour category insight sprint look like?

Here is a sprint a four-person pitch team can run between the brief landing and the first draft. The timings are a guide; the order matters more.

  1. Hours 0–4: define the battlefield. Agree the client's direct competitors, one or two challengers from adjacent categories and the markets that matter. Write the client's current claim in one sentence.
  2. Hours 4–16: read the category's content. Pull competitors' paid ads and organic posts. Tag each by claim, emotional territory, format and audience. Look for the territory everyone crowds into.
  3. Hours 16–24: listen to the audience. Cluster public conversation about the category into personas and tensions. Note the exact words people use, especially in complaints.
  4. Hours 24–32: find the unclaimed angle. Lay the audience's tensions against competitors' claims. The gap, a tension people talk about that no brand addresses, is your strategic platform.
  5. Hours 32–40: stress-test the evidence. Trace every claim in the deck back to real ads and posts. When the client's team challenges a slide, you want to show the source, not defend an opinion.
  6. Hours 40–48: build the story. One page on the category as it stands, one on the gap, one idea that fills it and the first test you would run. Credentials go at the back.

Which market research tools help agencies pitch faster?

The tool question is really a question about speed and trust. Most pitch teams use some mix of these:

  • Social listening for audience conversation and sentiment.
  • Ad libraries and competitor ad intelligence for what rivals are paying to say.
  • Survey panels for quantified answers to specific questions, if the timeline allows fieldwork.
  • Search and trend tools for demand signals.
  • AI assistants connected to your data, so the team can ask questions instead of building reports.

Judge any option on four tests. Can it cover a whole category in hours? Can every conclusion be traced to a source? Can non-specialists query it? Does it cost less than the pitch is worth? A survey-first approach usually fails the first test inside a 48-hour window.

What should independents learn from Publicis going direct to CEOs?

That the conversation has moved up a level. Sadoun is selling to chief executives worried about market share; Wieser is reminding everyone that spend follows competitive intensity. Both are talking about the client's competitive position, not about advertising.

An independent agency cannot buy a technology consultancy to get into the chief executive's office. It can walk into any pitch knowing more about the client's competitors than the client's own team does, and say so in language a CEO cares about: share, threats and the opening a rival has left. When the brief lands, those are the 48 hours that count.

Frequently asked questions

What should the first slide of an agency pitch be?

Something about the client's world, not yours. A single observation about their category, such as the territory every competitor crowds into or a customer tension nobody addresses, earns attention faster than an agency overview. Save credentials, process and team slides for the back, where they answer the question "can you deliver this?" rather than open the conversation.

What market research can an agency do in 48 hours?

Enough to form a defensible point of view. In two days a small team can review competitors' paid and organic content, cluster public audience conversation into personas and tensions, and identify an unclaimed angle, provided the data collection is automated. What 48 hours rarely allows is primary fieldwork such as surveys, so propose that research rather than pretend you have done it.

How do independent agencies beat holding companies in pitches?

By being sharper rather than bigger. Holding companies can offer scale, data partnerships and consulting arms; an independent can offer senior attention, speed and a more specific read of the client's category. Showing the client its competitors' content and its customers' words, traced to sources, is a credible way to make that case without matching a network's resources.

Is it worth pitching when the client already has a preferred agency?

Sometimes, if you qualify hard first. Digiday reports Publicis's chief executive arguing that clients increasingly focus on one preferred agency rather than reviewing everyone, and the group has won major accounts without a pitch. Ask about selection criteria, the investment level and who decides. If the answers are vague, the review may be a price check, and your best thinking may be wasted on it.
