Imposter syndrome in agencies: rebuilding confidence in the AI era

Constant judgement, perfectionism, juniors wondering what they are for now that AI is here: how to swap bravado for evidence in agency life.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 6 min read Also available in: English中文Français
The question behind this article

“What if my work isn't good enough? How do I deal with imposter syndrome in an agency, now that AI does part of my job?”

"What if my work isn't good enough?" In agencies, that question comes up far more often than anyone admits, and AI has given it fresh fuel. Short answer: imposter syndrome thrives wherever work is judged constantly and rarely measured. You defuse it by backing your ideas with verifiable evidence, by making questions normal and, at leadership level, by redesigning junior roles rather than cutting them.

The context doesn't help. At a town hall run by Digiday at the end of September, the owner of a small-to-mid-sized performance agency admitted: "We haven't hired college-level grads in some time, and I don't anticipate that we will be." AI now handles much of the work once handed to beginners. Start your career in that climate and doubting your place becomes almost rational.

Why is imposter syndrome so common in agencies?

Because everything is judgement. A recommendation has to be defended in front of a client who can sweep it aside in thirty seconds. A campaign goes past the creative director, then the client, then the public. The criteria for success change from one brief to the next, and the all-nighter before a pitch passes for a rite of passage.

Psychologists talk about the imposter phenomenon: the conviction that you don't deserve your place, that you succeeded by luck or by mistake, and the constant fear of being found out. It tends to affect capable, demanding people, hence its close link with perfectionism. It thrives when the value of a piece of work is fuzzy, because nothing contradicts the little voice whispering "you got lucky".

Yet the opposite is what works. Authenticity thrives where insecurity ebbs: once you no longer need to perform confidence, you finally dare to give your real point of view. And that point of view is what clients pay for.

Does AI make the doubt worse?

It adds a layer. The classic doubt, "am I good enough?", now comes with a new one: "if a machine can produce this brief in a minute, what am I for?". For a junior it is harsher still, since the tasks on which people used to learn the trade (summaries, monitoring, first drafts) are exactly the ones AI absorbs.

Digiday also cites a Pew Research Center survey finding that, in many countries, 18-to-34-year-olds worry more than their elders about losing their jobs to AI. At the same session, one participant put it like this: it is not AI that will take your job, it is someone who has mastered AI and has your experience.

Not everyone endorses the hiring freeze, though. Another participant quoted by Digiday sees it as short-sighted: when the seniors leave, the expertise will be missing. For him, it is a training challenge, not a technology one. The answer is to redesign junior roles, not to cut them.

Bravado or evidence: two ways to look confident

Faced with doubt, many agencies have long fallen back on posture: talk loudly, never say "I don't know". That holds up until the first trick question. Confidence built on evidence is less spectacular, but far sturdier.

How do you rebuild confidence in practice?

  1. Define "good enough" before you start. Agree with your manager what is expected from the deliverable. Perfectionism feeds on vague expectations.
  2. Source every claim. An idea grounded in observed behaviour can be defended without a tremor. It is the best antidote to "what if I'm wrong?".
  3. Use AI as a sparring partner. Ask it for the objections a client might raise, then prepare your answers. It prepares; you decide.
  4. Keep an evidence log. Each week, note what worked and why. The imposter's memory wipes out successes; paper doesn't.
  5. Ask for specific feedback. "Was it OK?" teaches you nothing. "What would make this recommendation more convincing?" changes everything.
  6. Get help if the doubt is wearing you down. When anxiety spills over into your sleep or your health, talk to a professional. It is not an admission of weakness.

What agency leaders need to change

Imposter syndrome is not just an individual matter. A shop's culture either feeds it or defuses it. The ideas raised at the session Digiday reported on are simple: pair seniors with juniors so they learn from each other, meet each person where they are, inspire with quick wins, be clear about the pace of change, then train.

Add one rule of your own: make questions normal. In an agency where asking is seen as weakness, everyone bluffs. In an agency where people check things together, everyone gets better.

Frequently asked questions

Is imposter syndrome an illness?

No. The imposter phenomenon describes a thought pattern, not a recognised psychiatric disorder: the feeling of not deserving your success and the fear of being found out. It can, however, come with anxiety or exhaustion. If the doubt is affecting your sleep, health or personal life over a long period, talk to a doctor or a psychologist.

Will AI replace agency juniors?

It mainly replaces tasks. According to Digiday, some agencies have stopped hiring graduates, while other professionals see that choice as short-sighted, because the expertise will be missing when the seniors leave. The sturdier path is to redesign junior roles around analysis, verification and client relationships.

Is using AI at work cheating?

No, as long as you keep the judgement and the responsibility for the result. The problem is approving without reading, or presenting as verified a conclusion you haven't checked. Used to prepare, explore and verify, AI strengthens your work. The client pays for your point of view, not for the time spent typing.

How can a manager help a team member who is full of self-doubt?

By clarifying expectations before the work rather than after, giving specific feedback and making questions normal in meetings. Pairing a senior with a junior, as participants quoted by Digiday suggested, also helps: each brings what the other lacks. And by highlighting concrete successes, the manager counters the imposter's tendency to forget them.
