Will AI replace marketing agencies? Why the maths rarely adds up

An AI subscription plus a hire looks cheaper than a retainer until you do the sums. Where AI genuinely replaces agency work, and where it frees people to do better work.

The question behind this article

“Will AI replace my marketing agency, or can it actually help me be more creative and efficient?”

Short answerMostly not. AI replaces agency tasks such as campaign set-up, QA, report assembly and monitoring, but not accountability, relationships or judgement. Once you add the people, tools and management needed to run AI in-house, the saving often disappears. AI does threaten agencies that charge premium fees for admin. Used well, it frees agency teams for more creative, higher-value work.

Key takeaways

  • AI replaces agency tasks, not agency accountability: set-up, QA, reporting and monitoring are the most exposed work.
  • Mumbrella's illustrative costing sets a $120,000 retainer against $100,000 to $120,000 for an AI subscription plus one hire, before on-costs and tools.
  • Agencies that sell administrative process as expertise are the ones most at risk from AI.
  • Clients trust people they can question and hold to account, which is why AI output still needs a named human sign-off.
  • AI only makes teams more creative if the hours it saves are ring-fenced for creative and client work in advance.

Will AI replace marketing agencies? For most clients, not on the numbers. AI replaces tasks: set-up, QA, assembly, summarising. It does not replace accountability, relationships or judgement, and once you price those in, the "cheaper" in-house AI option often costs more. Where AI does win is against agencies that sell admin as expertise. That distinction also answers the quieter question underneath: whether AI makes you more creative or simply redundant.

The debate got a proper costing this week. Writing in Mumbrella, AZK Media founder Azadeh Williams takes on the claim that an AI platform plus an in-house hire is cheaper than a PR agency. Her illustrative comparison sets a $10,000-a-month retainer, about $120,000 a year, against a $20,000 AI subscription plus an $80,000 to $100,000 PR hire before employment costs. Add media databases, monitoring and specialist support, and she argues a sophisticated in-house set-up can "easily reach north of $220,000."

What does the maths actually say?

The trap is comparing the price of a tool with the cost of a team. As Williams puts it, "you can't compare the price of one software subscription with the cost of an entire agency and call it a saving." The subscription is the cheapest line on the spreadsheet. The expensive lines are the person who runs it, the person who checks it, the tools around it and the senior judgement nobody budgeted for.

Run the sum yourself. The subscription plus one hire comes to roughly $100,000 to $120,000 a year before on-costs, extra tools or management time. That is already level with the retainer, and the client has swapped a team's range for one person's. If that person is off sick, resigns or simply does not know the press in a new market, the saving evaporates.

Williams is writing about PR, but the logic travels. Swap "PR hire" for "performance marketer" or "social lead" and the shape of the sum looks much the same.

Where does the maths work?

Williams is not defending every agency. She argues that agencies charging high fees for generic releases, recycled pitches and clippings-based reporting are vulnerable, and that "clients shouldn't be paying premium fees for administrative inefficiency." That is the honest answer to the headline question. AI will replace agencies whose value was mostly process.

A Forbes Agency Council panel of agency leaders lands in a similar place. Multiply's Matt Jayson singles out campaign set-up and QA, loading and checking ads inside the platforms, as the agency work that gets automated: "For one high-growth client, that's easily 20 hours a week of invisible work." Pierce Kafka of Kafka Media Group argues that as reports become automatic, clients will need "human interpretation that explains why the data matters, what it reveals and what to do next."

Now look at the chores most agencies quietly loathe: timesheets, monthly reports, status meetings, budget tracking, manual resizing, invoice chasing, competitor audits. None of them is why a client hired you. All of them are why your people go home late.

Why don't clients trust AI on its own?

Psychologists who study trust tend to break it into three questions. Is the other party able? Do they mean well? Will they be straight with me? Trust Theory, in plain words, says we only rely on someone when all three answers are yes.

An algorithm can demonstrate ability. It struggles with the other two. It cannot mean well, and it cannot be held to account when it is wrong. That is why a client will happily let AI draft a hundred headlines but wants a named person to sign off the one that runs. It is also why Williams argues that AI can help find journalists and analyse coverage, while only people build the trust that wins editorial opportunities. Trust is bred in conversation, not in the data of algorithms.

The same holds inside the agency. Teams distrust AI outputs they cannot question. They start trusting them when they can interrogate the data like a colleague: ask a follow-up, challenge a conclusion, see the posts behind a claim. That is the thinking behind Defy MCP, which lets teams question client and category data inside Claude, prompting it like a co-worker rather than learning another dashboard.

Can AI make you more creative, not just faster?

Yes, but only if you decide what the time is for. Saved hours have a habit of turning into extra meetings. Here is a straightforward way to make sure they turn into better work.

  1. Log a fortnight of real work. Every task, every person, in half-hour blocks.
  2. Sort each task into chore, craft or relationship. Be honest. A surprising amount of "strategy" turns out to be formatting.
  3. Automate one chore end to end. The monthly report is usually the best first candidate.
  4. Ring-fence the hours you get back. Assign them before you save them: creative development, client workshops, testing new ideas.
  5. Keep a named person on everything client-facing. AI drafts; people sign.
  6. Make outputs checkable. If the team cannot see where a conclusion came from, they will not trust it, and neither will the client.
  7. Tell the client what changed. Show them the hours that moved from chores to thinking. It is the strongest answer to "couldn't AI do this cheaper?"

What should you say when a client asks if AI could replace you?

Show them the maths, then show them your chore list. Agree that AI should do the repetitive work, because it should. Then be specific about what they are paying you for: judgement, relationships, accountability and ideas. If you cannot name those clearly, the client's question is a fair one.

Williams closes with a line worth borrowing: "The future isn't AI versus PR agencies, it's AI-powered PR agencies versus outdated PR models." Replace PR with your own discipline. That is the real contest, and it is one agencies can win. It is also the split behind the Defy platform: machines take the chores, people keep the judgement.

The insight
Trust is bred in the soil of conversation, not in the data of algorithms.

Trust Theory says we rely on others when we believe they are able, well-intentioned and honest. Algorithms can show ability, but trust in agencies, and in AI tools, still forms through conversation and accountability.

Frequently asked questions

Which agency jobs are most at risk from AI?

It is more accurate to talk about tasks than jobs. The most exposed work is repetitive and rule-based: campaign set-up and QA, report assembly, monitoring, resizing and first-draft copy. Roles built mainly around those tasks will change most. Roles centred on strategy, client relationships, creative judgement and accountability are far harder to automate.

Is it cheaper to replace an agency with AI and an in-house hire?

Usually not, once you count everything. In Mumbrella's illustrative example, an AI subscription plus one PR hire costs about as much as a $120,000 retainer before employment costs, tools and management time, and a sophisticated in-house set-up can pass $220,000. It can make sense for simple, high-volume work, but rarely for complex, multi-market programmes.

How can agencies use AI without losing client trust?

Be open about where AI is used, keep a named person accountable for everything the client sees and make AI conclusions checkable against their sources. Spend the time AI saves on visible, higher-value work such as creative development and strategic advice, and tell the client what changed. Trust falls when AI feels hidden and grows when it is explained.

Will AI replace creative agencies?

It is unlikely in the foreseeable future, though it will change what they sell. AI can generate volume, but clients still need someone to choose the right idea, protect the brand and take responsibility for the result. Creative agencies that shift from producing assets to shaping strategy and direction, as several Forbes Agency Council contributors suggest, are best placed.
