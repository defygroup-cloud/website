AI roles in agencies: stop making AI everyone's job

Giving every employee a code account and a build day leaves agencies with a mess. Three clear AI roles, builders, users and approvers, work better for people and for clients.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 7 min read Also available in: English中文
The question behind this article

“Will AI take my job in marketing, and does everyone in my agency really need to learn to build with it?”

Short answerNo. Everyone benefits from AI; not everyone needs to build it. Agencies that handed every employee a code account found half the team thrived and half didn't, leaving a mess of unmaintained tools. A better model uses three roles: builders who invest extra time, users who run the systems daily and report problems, and approvers who sign off quality before anything ships.

Key takeaways

  • Everyone benefits from AI, but not everyone needs to build it: blanket "everyone builds" mandates leave agencies with undocumented, unmaintained automations.
  • Three roles bring order: builders who dedicate extra time, users who run the systems daily and report problems, and approvers who sign off quality before anything ships.
  • Builders are not engineers; they are curious people with the appetite and the protected time to improve the agency's workflows.
  • Turning staff into part-engineer, part-expert, part-client-facing hybrids creates a hiring and succession problem the moment they leave.
  • Gartner found 18% of 1,303 senior marketing leaders have eliminated some roles because of AI and 16% have created new ones; clear roles give juniors a path rather than a closed door.

Will AI take your marketing job, and does everyone in your agency need to learn to build with it? The honest answer to the second question is no, and getting that right makes the first question far less frightening. Everyone benefits from AI. Not everyone needs to build it.

The anxiety is well founded. At a town hall during its AI Marketing Strategies event, executives admitted to Digiday that some employers are actively avoiding entry-level hires as AI absorbs junior work. One small-to-mid-sized performance agency owner said: "We haven't hired college-level grads in some time, and I don't anticipate that we will be." Meanwhile, Gartner research reported by Marketing Dive found that of 1,303 senior marketing leaders, 18% have already eliminated some positions because of AI and 16% have created new roles.

Faced with that, many agency leaders reach for the same fix: make everyone an AI builder. One agency founder tried exactly that, and told an AI adoption session in Singapore this week why he wouldn't do it again.

What happens when an agency makes AI everyone's job?

His agency of around 20 people gave everyone their own code account: strategists and creatives, but also HR and finance. They set aside 20% of the week for it. Fridays became "build Fridays".

The result split the team down the middle. Half got it. Half didn't. And the half who got it produced a sprawl of random skills and automations: one person's shortcut for a performance client, another's fix for a different account, none documented, none maintained, and nobody quite sure what triggered what.

Any agency person will recognise the pattern. It is the shared drive, except now it runs. Picture a media planner's bidding script that only works on her laptop, or a copywriter's prompt chain that lives in a personal account and leaves when he does.

What are builders, users and approvers?

The fix was to appoint roles. As the diagram shows, there are three, under one line from his slide: "Everyone benefits from AI. Not everyone needs to build it."

RoleWho they areWhat they doAgency example
BuildersNot engineers: people with passion who dedicate more timeDesign, build and improve the agency's AI workflows, and document themA systems-minded planner who turns the monthly media report into a repeatable workflow
UsersEveryone who works in the systems day to dayRun the work and feed back bugs and requestsAccount executives producing client updates who flag when an output looks wrong
ApproversSubject-matter experts who own quality controlCheck the output; nothing ships until they have signed it offThe head of strategy who reviews every insight deck before it reaches a client

The most important detail is the first one: builders are not engineers. They are the curious, patient people willing to give the time. In a creative shop that might be a producer. In a CRM agency, a data-minded campaign manager. In a PR firm, the account director who already wrote the agency's press-release templates.

Approvers matter just as much. They are the people who define what good looks like for the agency's work, and they are the reason a half-finished AI draft never lands in a client's inbox.

Why does "everyone builds" feed imposter syndrome?

The imposter phenomenon is the persistent feeling that you are less competent than others believe, and that sooner or later you will be found out. It thrives when the rules of competence change overnight.

A blanket "everyone builds" mandate does exactly that. A senior copywriter with fifteen years of craft is suddenly judged on whether she can wire up an automation. The half of the team who "didn't get it" don't feel freed from chores; they feel behind. The half who did get it start to wonder whether they are still strategists or have quietly become junior engineers.

Clear roles reverse the pressure. A user isn't failing at building; they are doing a defined, valuable job. An approver's experience becomes the agency's quality standard. That is the logic behind the insight: authenticity thrives where insecurity ebbs. People do their most honest work when they know what they are there for.

What happens when your AI hybrids leave?

The founder asked a question every agency MD should sit with. If you turn people into part-engineer, part-expert, part-client-facing hybrids, what happens when they leave? And what happens when you need to hire and scale?

You can't advertise for that person. The market doesn't produce account directors who also maintain your custom automations. When one resigns, the skills they built either break or become archaeology.

Roles solve the succession problem. Builders document what they build because other people depend on it. Users can be hired for their client or craft skills and trained on the system quickly. Approvers stay anchored in subject expertise, which is what clients pay for.

This matters for the junior question too. Gartner predicts most high-performing teams will eliminate traditional entry-level jobs by 2030, but it urges CMOs to give juniors higher-value work earlier rather than cut the pipeline. Defined roles give that advice a shape: a graduate can start as a user with clear feedback duties, then grow into a builder or, with time and judgement, an approver.

How do I assign AI roles in my agency?

  1. Drop the blanket mandate. Keep access for everyone; drop the expectation that everyone builds.
  2. Audit what has been built. List every skill, prompt chain and automation in use. Keep, merge or retire each one, and give every survivor an owner.
  3. Choose builders by appetite, not job title. Look for the people who already tinker. Give them protected time, rather than spreading the same slice across everybody.
  4. Name an approver for every workflow. A subject-matter expert who defines what good looks like and signs off before anything reaches a client.
  5. Give users one feedback channel. A single place to log bugs and requests, reviewed every week by the builders.
  6. Write the roles into job descriptions. Roles that live only in a Slack message disappear at the next reorganisation.

So is AI a threat to marketing job security?

To tasks, yes. To judgement, much less. The work most exposed is the chores: monthly reports, competitor audits, manual resizing, status meetings. The work least exposed is deciding what matters, what is good and what to tell the client.

Frequently asked questions

Do AI builders in an agency need to be engineers?

No. In the roles model shared at the Singapore session, builders are people with passion who dedicate more time and energy, not trained engineers. They design and improve the agency's AI workflows and document them so others can use them. Complex systems may still need engineering help, which is one reason many independents buy maintained platforms rather than building everything.

Who should be an approver?

A subject-matter expert who already knows what good looks like for that piece of work: a head of strategy for insight decks, a senior media lead for performance reports, a creative director for concepts. Approvers own quality control. Nothing produced with AI should ship to a client until they have checked it and signed it off.

Will AI replace entry-level agency roles?

It is changing them fast. Gartner predicts most high-performing marketing teams will eliminate traditional entry-level jobs by 2030, and Digiday heard from an agency owner who no longer hires graduates. Gartner's advice is to give juniors higher-value work earlier. Defined AI roles help: juniors can start as users with feedback duties and grow into builders or approvers.

Should every employee still get access to AI tools?

Generally, yes. Access lets everyone benefit from AI, from drafting to research. What changes is the expectation. Users should not be judged on building automations, and anything that reaches a client should pass through an approver. Keep access broad, keep building concentrated, and keep quality control with named experts.
