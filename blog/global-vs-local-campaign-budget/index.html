How to optimise marketing budget ROI when global campaigns underdeliver

Before cutting channels, check whether budget is backing creative that doesn't fit its market. A practical way to reallocate spend with evidence.

The question behind this article

“How can I optimise my marketing budget so that every pound I spend contributes to a better return?”

Short answerStart with creative fit, not media rates. Audit which assets run in each market, read local competitor and audience context, and score creative before buying media. Then move a ring-fenced share of low-confidence spend towards adapted or local work, with pre-agreed rules to scale or stop. How do you optimise a marketing budget to improve ROI? Before you trim channels or haggle over CPMs, check whether money is going behind creative that does not fit the market it runs in. Moving spend from unadapted global work towards adapted or local work you have tested before buying media is often the quickest return available, and the cost of not doing it never appears on an invoice.

Fresh evidence landed in September. Campaign reported that global campaigns deliver fewer commercial outcomes, based on a study by Publicis London, System1 and Effie comparing the effectiveness of global and local-market campaigns. The full write-up sits behind Campaign's paywall, so treat the headline as a direction rather than a formula. It is still a direction most budget holders have not acted on.

What did the global versus local research find?

Here is what is public. The study was run by Publicis London, System1 and Effie. It compared the effectiveness of global campaigns with local-market campaigns. And, in Campaign's words, global campaigns "deliver fewer commercial outcomes".

That matters because centralised creative is usually sold on efficiency. One idea, one shoot, one set of assets, rolled out across markets with a translated end frame. The production saving is visible on day one. The effectiveness cost, if this research holds, is spread across every market and every month the campaign runs, and nobody books it.

Before you quote the study in a client deck, read the full version for its sample, categories and how "commercial outcomes" were defined. A headline is not a business case.

Why do teams keep funding campaigns that underperform?

Inaction can be the greatest loss of all. It is an uncomfortable line for budget holders, because inaction rarely feels like a decision.

Prospect theory, developed by the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, explains why. People judge options against a reference point, usually the current plan, and feel potential losses more sharply than equivalent gains. Changing the allocation creates a visible, attributable risk: if the local adaptation flops, someone signed it off. Keeping the global plan creates an invisible loss: money that quietly earns less than it could.

So the default wins. Global stays global, last year's split rolls forward, and the underperformance becomes the baseline everyone measures against. The fix is not braver people. It is making the cost of standing still as visible as the cost of change.

Where does marketing budget actually leak?

In most plans the leaks are not in the media rates, which procurement has usually squeezed already. They are in fit.

  • Unadapted creative running in markets where the idea, references or humour do not land.
  • Media weight behind weak assets, because the buy was booked before anyone knew how the creative would perform.
  • Even spreading across markets and channels, so no market gets enough to matter.
  • Assets produced and never run, because they were made to a global template nobody locally wanted.

How do I reallocate budget to improve ROI?

  1. Audit spend by market and asset. For each market, list which creative is running, whether it was adapted, and what it cost to place.
  2. Read each market's context. Look at what local competitors are running and how audiences talk about the category. This is where global plans are usually blind.
  3. Score creative before you buy media. Pre-test global and adapted versions with local audiences or predictive scoring, so weak assets are fixed before they absorb budget.
  4. Ring-fence a test budget. Take a fixed slice from your lowest-confidence spend and fund adapted or local work with it. Small enough to survive a failure, large enough to read.
  5. Agree the rules up front. Decide what result scales a test, what kills it and when you will look. Pre-agreed rules take the politics out of moving money.
  6. Put the cost of inaction in the report. Next to every "keep", state what you expect it to deliver against the best alternative. Make standing still a line item.
  7. Reallocate on a fixed rhythm. Monthly or quarterly, move money towards what is working. An annual plan that never moves is a bet placed once and never checked.

How do you sell reallocation to a global client or a CFO?

Frame it as risk reduction, not creative preference. You are not arguing that local is better in principle. You are arguing that buying media against creative nobody has checked in that market is the riskier choice, and that new evidence points the same way.

Lead with one market, one test and pre-agreed rules. Use the global versus local research as context, not proof. And bring local evidence: what competitors are saying in that market and how the audience talks about the category. Local teams often know this instinctively. Your job is to turn instinct into something a finance director will sign.

Imagine a brand running one global film across eight markets. Rather than argue for eight local campaigns, propose pre-scoring the film in its two largest growth markets, adapting it where it scores poorly, and comparing results after a quarter. That is a small, bounded change with a clear readout, which is much easier to approve than a new strategy.

Frequently asked questions

What is the quickest way to improve marketing ROI?

Look for spend going behind weak or ill-fitting creative before you cut channels. Media rates have usually been negotiated hard already, while creative fit is rarely checked market by market before the buy. Scoring assets before launch, adapting the ones that do not fit and moving money towards proven work is often a bigger lever than another round of rate negotiations.

Are global campaigns less effective than local ones?

New research suggests they can be. Campaign reported in September 2026 that a study by Publicis London, System1 and Effie, comparing global and local-market campaigns, found global campaigns deliver fewer commercial outcomes. The detail is behind Campaign's paywall, so check the methodology before applying it. It does not mean global ideas never work; it means unadapted ones deserve testing market by market.

What is creative pre-testing or pre-scoring?

Pre-testing means checking how a piece of creative is likely to perform before you spend media money on it. Traditional methods use surveys or panels; newer approaches use models that predict outcomes such as clickability from the asset itself. Either way, the aim is the same: fix or drop weak assets before they absorb budget, and back strong ones with confidence.

How much budget should I hold back for testing?

There is no universal figure. Take it from your lowest-confidence spend, such as unadapted creative in secondary markets, and size it so a failed test is affordable but a successful one is readable. What matters more than the percentage is agreeing in advance what result will scale the test, what will stop it, and when you will decide.
