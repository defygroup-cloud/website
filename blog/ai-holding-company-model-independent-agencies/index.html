AI and the end of the holding company model: lessons for independent agencies

WPP is set to cut another 1,000 roles and Gartner forecasts agency fees at 15% of marketing budgets by 2030. What the dismantling of the pyramid teaches independent agencies.

The question behind this article

“Will AI kill the advertising holding company model? And what should my independent agency do about it?”

Short answerNot overnight, but it's already changing it. Cuts at WPP and other groups show the junior-heavy pyramid giving way to lean, senior teams that orchestrate AI. For an independent agency, the advantage is having little structure and no legacy technology to pay for: you can adopt the same analytical capability without the cost of building it.

Key takeaways

  • According to the FT, as reported by The Drum, WPP is set to cut around 1,000 more roles; its headcount fell 6.4% in a year, to 97,388.
  • Gartner estimates that agency fees have fallen to 19.2% of marketing budgets and forecasts 15% by 2030.
  • AI is inverting the agency pyramid: fewer juniors on execution, more senior teams orchestrating systems.
  • Independents, with less overhead and less legacy technology, can buy in the analytical capability the networks built in-house.
  • Automate the task, not the judgement, and don't close the door on juniors.

Will AI kill the advertising holding company model? Not overnight, but the dismantling has already begun, and it opens up space for independent agencies. The pyramid of juniors doing manual work is giving way to lean, senior teams that orchestrate AI. Those with less structure and no legacy technology to pay for adapt faster.

The latest signal came from WPP. According to a Financial Times report picked up by The Drum, the group is set to cut around 1,000 more roles in the coming months. Since the start of 2025, nearly 11,000 positions have already gone. Headcount ended June at 97,388, down 6.4% year on year.

What's happening to the holding companies?

WPP isn't alone. The same report notes that Omnicom announced around 4,000 job cuts after completing its acquisition of IPG, and that Dentsu is pressing ahead with eliminating roughly 3,400 roles outside Japan, about 8% of its international workforce. WPP, for its part, is chasing £500m in annual savings by 2028.

The backdrop is financial. According to Jay Wilson, the Gartner analyst quoted by The Drum, marketing budgets have stalled at 7.8% of company revenue. Within them, the share going to agency fees has been falling since 2023 and now stands at 19.2%; Gartner forecasts 15% by 2030. Working media, meanwhile, takes 31.4% of budgets, the highest share Gartner has ever recorded.

In other words: clients aren't spending less on marketing. They're spending less on agencies.

Why does AI threaten the holding company model in particular?

Because the model was built on scale of people. Lots of juniors, lots of hours, lots of manual production: monthly reports, competitor audits, resizing assets, gathering data. That was how margin was made and how talent was trained.

AI eats precisely that base. Wilson describes the pyramid inverting: fewer people on execution, more senior teams running AI systems, with the cuts concentrated at entry level. In his words, "the often-bloated hold co model isn't holding up to AI impacts".

And here's the part that matters if you're not a holding company. According to Wilson, independents, with less overhead and fewer sunk technology costs, are better placed to benefit. "AI is helping scale the capabilities of those independent agencies," he told The Drum.

Does technology alone decide the future of agencies?

Technological determinism is the idea that technology drives social and organisational change, not the other way round. The printing press, television and the internet redrew advertising whether the industry liked it or not. AI joins the same list.

The useful version of this theory for an agency isn't the fatalistic one. It's recognising that the model will change regardless, and that the only real choice is who designs the change: you, or your client's procurement team.

Amid the chaos of cuts, mergers and AI announcements, clarity emerges when you connect data to humanity. Technology does the groundwork. People decide what it means for a brand, a category, an audience. That division of labour is the new org chart.

The table simplifies, of course. There are nimble holding companies and hidebound independents. But the structural trend favours those who are small and decide quickly.

What can independent agencies learn right now?

  1. Take an inventory of tasks. List what the team does every week: timesheets, monthly reports, status meetings, competitor audits, budget tracking. Mark what's repetitive.
  2. Automate the task, not the judgement. AI on collection, consolidation and first-pass analysis. People on interpretation, ideas and client conversations.
  3. Buy capability, don't build it. The holding companies spent millions on proprietary stacks. An independent can get equivalent analytical capability by subscribing to a platform, with no development cost and no maintenance burden.
  4. Reinvest the hours you free up. In strategy, in new business, in category depth. Not in more of the same production.
  5. Rethink how you charge. If fees are shrinking as a share of budgets, selling hours means selling exactly what AI makes cheaper. Price delivery and outcomes instead.
  6. Don't abandon your juniors. The inverted pyramid leaves an awkward question: where will the seniors of 2030 learn their trade? Use AI to speed up newcomers' learning, not to close the door on them.

And in Brazil, when does this arrive?

The Drum's report contains no figures for Brazil, and it would be reckless to make them up. But the logic doesn't need a passport. The holding companies operate there, global clients buy there, and the pressure on the fee share arrives through the same procurement departments.

For an independent agency in Brazil, the message is practical. The big competitor will become leaner and more automated, and size stops being an automatic advantage. What's left as a differentiator is speed, closeness to the client and quality of thinking. Exactly what a well-equipped independent knows how to deliver.

That's why AI for independent agencies isn't about having the most sophisticated model or an innovation lab to show off on the website. It's about taking off the team's desks the tasks the holding companies handled with sheer headcount, and putting those hours where independents have always been better: the idea, the client and the category.

The insight
In connecting data to humanity, clarity emerges from chaos.

Technological determinism holds that technology drives organisational change, whether organisations like it or not. AI is redrawing the holding company model; the choice for independents is to use the machine for the groundwork and keep people on the meaning.

Frequently asked questions

Will advertising holding companies disappear because of AI?

Nothing points to an imminent end, but the model is being reshaped. The Drum reports cuts at WPP, Omnicom and Dentsu, and Gartner forecasts that agency fees' share of marketing budgets will fall from 19.2% to 15% by 2030. The likeliest outcome is that holding companies become smaller, more senior and more automated, rather than disappearing.

Why can independent agencies benefit from AI?

Because they have less overhead, less legacy technology to write off and faster decision-making. According to Gartner's Jay Wilson, speaking to The Drum, that leaves them better placed, since AI is helping to scale their capabilities. In practice, an independent can buy in the analytical capability the networks built internally, without paying for the development.

Which agency tasks should AI take on first?

The repetitive, low-judgement ones: monthly reports, consolidating campaign data, competitor audits, budget tracking, resizing assets and status updates. These tasks eat hours and rarely set an agency apart. Strategy, the creative idea, data interpretation and the client relationship remain people's work.

What happens to junior training with an inverted pyramid?

It's the least discussed risk. If AI takes over the execution that used to train juniors, the agency needs to create another learning path: more time alongside seniors, critical review of AI-assisted work and involvement in analysis and strategy from early on. Closing the entry door saves money now and leaves the agency without seniors later.
