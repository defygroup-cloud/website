AI impact on agency jobs: how to rebuild the junior career ladder

Gartner and Digiday say AI is hollowing out entry-level agency work. Cutting the graduate pipeline is the wrong answer. Redesigning the junior role is the right one.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 8 min read

The question behind this article

“How will AI affect jobs in my agency, including mine, and how do I prepare my team?”

Short answerAI is removing task-shaped agency work, not the need for people. Gartner found 18% of senior marketing leaders have cut some roles because of AI, while 16% have created new ones. Reporting, audits and resizing are most exposed. The best preparation is redesigning junior roles around judgement – questioning data, checking AI output and linking insight to outcomes – rather than halting graduate hiring.

Key takeaways

  • Gartner's survey of 1,303 senior marketing leaders found 18% have already eliminated some positions because of AI, 16% have created new roles and nearly a third have reworked existing functions.
  • The agency work most exposed to AI is task-shaped – monthly reporting, competitor audits, manual resizing and meeting admin – rather than judgement, taste or client relationships.
  • Stopping graduate hiring saves money this year and creates a shortage of experienced account and strategy people in five years' time.
  • Juniors whose value was speed at grunt work are prone to imposter feelings when AI takes that work; making critical questioning of data and AI output their explicit job restores confidence.
  • The most practical redesign moves juniors from fetching data to interrogating it, with seniors coaching the judgement.

If you run or work in an agency, the honest answer to "will AI take our jobs?" is this: it is taking tasks, and the tasks it takes first are the ones we used to give juniors. That makes the entry-level role the pressure point. The agencies that come out of this well will redesign junior jobs around judgement, not quietly stop hiring graduates.

The quiet part is now being said out loud. At a town hall during its AI Marketing Strategies event, Digiday heard the owner of a small-to-mid-sized performance agency say: "We haven't hired college-level grads in some time, and I don't anticipate that we will be." A day after Digiday published, Marketing Dive reported Gartner's prediction that most high-performing marketing teams will eliminate traditional entry-level jobs by 2030.

Is AI actually taking agency jobs?

Some, yes. The numbers are less apocalyptic than the headlines and more specific. Gartner surveyed 1,303 senior marketing leaders between January and April 2026. According to Marketing Dive, 18% had already eliminated some positions because of AI. Sixteen per cent had created new roles. Nearly a third had reworked existing functions.

Look at which number is biggest. Reworking. Most organisations are not deleting departments; they are changing what the people in them do. The jobs that disappear are the ones that were really a bundle of repeatable tasks with a title stapled on.

The agency owner quoted by Digiday was not anti-graduate. His firm used to hire only entry-level talent, and most of those hires became among its best employees. "It's just a business issue," he said. That is the trap: a decision that is rational for one year's P&L and corrosive for the next decade's talent bench.

Which agency roles are most exposed to AI?

Stop thinking in job titles and start thinking in tasks. Every agency has a list of chores that eat junior hours: timesheets, monthly reports, status meetings, budget tracking, manual resizing, competitor audits. Those are exactly the chores AI and automation now handle well. Judgement, taste and client trust are not on that list.

Here is how the work shifts for a typical junior in a creative, media or social agency.

Junior task todayWhat AI now doesWhat the junior should do instead
Building the monthly performance reportPulls the numbers and drafts the commentarySpot the one change that matters and explain it to the client
Competitor audit by screenshotCollects and groups competitor ads and postsDecide which competitor move is a threat and which is noise
Social listening summaryClusters conversation into themesTest whether a theme is a real audience tension or a loud minority
Resizing assets for every formatGenerates the variantsCheck brand fit and catch what looks wrong
Status meeting notes and actionsTranscribes the call and lists actionsChase the decision that is blocking the work

The right-hand column is harder than the middle one. It is also more interesting, more visible to clients and a much faster way to learn the business.

Why is cutting graduate hiring a false economy?

Today's account executive is 2032's account director. Stop hiring at the bottom and in five years you will be buying experienced people at the top, bidding against holding networks with deeper pockets. For an independent agency, that is a bad trade.

Gartner's advice to CMOs, as reported by Marketing Dive, applies just as well to agency leaders: do not use AI efficiencies as a reason to cut new talent out of the pipeline. Kristina LaRocca-Cerrone, a vice president analyst at Gartner, put it simply: "CMOs should give junior marketers exposure to higher-value work earlier." Gartner's examples are useful ones: evaluating AI outputs, generating insights and linking recommendations to measurable outcomes.

One attendee at the Digiday town hall framed it better than most consultants could: "It seems like a learning and development challenge, not a technology challenge."

Why does AI make capable people feel like frauds?

There is a psychological layer here that most AI roadmaps ignore. Psychologists call it the imposter phenomenon: the persistent sense that you are less competent than people think and about to be found out, whatever the evidence says.

Agencies are prime territory for it. Juniors often prove their worth through speed and stamina on grunt work. Take that work away and many feel they have nothing left to show. Seniors are not immune. One participant at the Digiday event summed up the fear: "AI isn't coming to replace your job. Someone who knows AI and has your experience is coming to replace your job."

People who feel like frauds behave in predictable ways. They over-polish instead of shipping. They don't ask the obvious question in the meeting. They hide uncertainty rather than flag it, which is the last thing you want from someone checking AI output. Authenticity thrives where insecurity ebbs. Give people a clear, valued role and the tools to answer their own questions, and they start offering their real opinions.

How do you redesign a junior agency role for AI?

Start small: one team, one client, one quarter.

  1. Audit the chores. List every recurring task juniors do in a month and the hours each one takes. Mark the ones AI or automation can now handle.
  2. Rewrite the job description around questions, not outputs. "Produces the monthly report" becomes "explains what changed for the client, and why".
  3. Make checking the machine a named skill. Give juniors explicit responsibility for verifying AI output against the source data, and credit them when they catch an error.
  4. Give every junior a weekly client question. Something real, such as "why did engagement on the competitor's new campaign jump?", answered with evidence and presented to a senior.
  5. Pair juniors with seniors. One Digiday participant noted that older staff hold "historical knowledge about like just how the sauce gets made". Juniors bring fluency with the tools. Trade both ways.
  6. Hire for judgement. Gartner suggests assessing candidates on how they work with AI, including their critical thinking. Set a task built on an AI-generated analysis with a deliberate mistake in it, and see who spots it.

What should agency leaders tell their teams now?

The truth, with a plan attached. Telling people nothing will change is not credible. Telling them AI will do everything is not true. One executive at the Digiday event described a sensible sequence: quick wins first, then clarity about the pace of change, then formal training.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI replace account executives at agencies?

AI will replace much of what account executives currently spend time on, such as compiling reports, writing up meeting notes and tracking budgets. It will not replace the need for someone who understands the client, spots problems early and keeps work moving. The role is likely to shrink in admin and grow in judgement, so account executives who learn to question data and AI output become more valuable, not less.

What skills should graduates build to get hired by an agency in the AI era?

Critical thinking with AI tools matters most. Gartner advises assessing candidates on how they work with AI, including their judgement. In practice that means framing a sharp question, checking an AI-generated answer against its source, explaining why a number moved and turning that into a recommendation a client can act on. Clear writing and confident presenting still count.

Is it cheaper to replace junior staff with AI tools?

In the short term, often yes, because AI can absorb routine tasks. Over a few years it usually costs more. Agencies that stop hiring at entry level eventually have to buy experienced account and strategy people on the open market, where independents compete with larger networks on salary. Redesigning junior roles keeps the pipeline and still captures the efficiency.

How do I stop my team feeling threatened by AI?

Be specific about which tasks are changing and which skills the agency will value more. Start with quick wins that remove obvious chores, explain the expected pace of change, then invest in formal training. Give people named responsibility for checking and challenging AI output, so their judgement is visibly part of the process rather than something the tool replaces.
