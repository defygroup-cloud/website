AI marketing tools for agencies: build a stack, not a shopping list

Most agencies don't have an AI stack. They have a pile of clever tools that don't talk to each other. Here's how to judge AI marketing tools on the thing that matters: integration.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 8 min read Also available in: English中文
The question behind this article

“Which AI marketing tools should my agency use, and how do I make sure they actually work with the systems we already have?”

Short answerChoose AI marketing tools by how well they connect, not by their feature lists. Start from the two or three workflows that eat the most hours, map where the data has to flow, and pick tools that share one source of truth, show their working and can be written into client contracts. A connected stack of three beats a dozen clever apps that never talk.

Key takeaways

  • The value of an agency AI stack comes from how its tools connect, not from how many tools it contains.
  • Campaign, reporting on the Ingenuity Pulse study, says only one in three marketers is satisfied with the results of their martech investments, a gap that puts agency relationships at risk.
  • Digiday reports that agencies are adding AI terms to existing contracts clause by clause, usually when a client wants tool access, asks for AI language or a new AI tool touches their project.
  • Before buying another tool, map one workflow end to end and find every point where data is re-keyed, exported or lost.
  • Every tool in the stack should be explainable to a client: what it does, what data it touches and how its conclusions can be checked.

Which AI marketing tools should an agency use? Start with a different question: which tools will work with what you already run, and produce something a client can see, check and pay for? For an agency, integration is the feature. Everything else is a demo.

Clients are asking a version of the same thing. Campaign, reporting on the Ingenuity Pulse study, says only one in three marketers is satisfied with the results of their martech investments, and frames that value gap as a threat to agency retention. Meanwhile Digiday reports that agencies are patching AI into existing master service agreements clause by clause. "We are at the point now where we are just simply working that into existing MSAs," Mile Marker's CEO told the publication. Stacks, like contracts, are being bolted together on the fly.

Why do agencies end up with a pile of AI tools instead of a stack?

Because every purchase made sense at the time. Picture a 40-person independent. Strategy bought a listening tool for a pitch. The social team uses a separate ad library. Everyone has a chatbot seat and uses it differently. Someone added a reporting connector, the studio loves its image generator, and the project management software now has an AI button nobody presses.

Each tool is fine. Together they produce exports, copy-and-paste and five versions of the truth. The monthly report is still assembled by hand on the last Thursday of the month, and the competitor audit still lives in a deck someone made in March.

The holding networks dealt with this by hiring data and AI teams to wire everything together. Independents rarely have that team, so the wiring falls to whoever is "good with tech", usually on top of a full client load.

Why it keeps happening: a systems problem, not a tools problem

Systems theory offers the plainest explanation. A system's results come from the relationships between its parts, not from the parts on their own. A brilliant violinist and a brilliant drummer who cannot hear each other do not make an orchestra. They make noise.

In an agency, the relationships are handoffs: listening data into the strategy deck, the deck into the brief, the brief into creative, creative into media, media into the report. Every handoff that depends on someone exporting a spreadsheet is a place where insight leaks and hours disappear.

The arithmetic is unkind. Five tools have ten possible connections between them. Ten tools have 45. Adding a clever new app does not add one capability; it adds a set of new handoffs that someone has to manage. That is why a bigger stack so often feels slower than a smaller one.

What should I look for when evaluating AI marketing tools?

Judge each tool by how it behaves inside your system, not by its feature list. These are the questions worth asking before a demo turns into a contract.

The contract row matters more than it used to. Digiday found that clients now ask for terms on human oversight, indemnity, tool disclosure and ownership of AI-generated work. As Reed Smith partner Keri Bruce put it: "Ultimately, people want disclosure. They want to know what tools are being used." A tool you cannot explain to a client is a tool you cannot safely use on their account.

How do I build an AI stack that works with my existing systems?

Work from the workflow outwards, not from the vendor list inwards. A practical sequence:

  1. Pick the two workflows that hurt most. Monthly reports, competitor audits, status meetings and manual resizing are the usual suspects. Log the hours they take for one month so you have a baseline.
  2. Draw the data path. On one page, trace where the data starts, every system it touches and every point where a person copies it from one place to another. Those points are your integration targets.
  3. Choose a spine. Decide on one intelligence layer where client, competitor and audience data lives. Other tools read from it rather than keeping their own copies.
  4. Put the AI where people already work. A tool your team has to log into separately will be ignored by Friday. An assistant that can query your data from inside the tools they already use gets adopted.
  5. Automate one chore end to end. Turning one monthly report from a manual slog into an automated draft that a person checks beats shaving ten per cent off ten tasks.
  6. Write it into the paperwork. Use Digiday's three triggers as a checklist: a client wants a seat in your AI tool, a client asks for AI language, or a new AI tool is used on their project. Have a standard addendum ready for each.
  7. Retire something every quarter. Every tool must justify its connections. If nobody would notice it had gone, switch it off.

Should independent agencies build their own AI stack or buy one?

Some agencies are building. Digiday describes agencies moving towards a software-style model through proprietary tools, which changes how they get paid. VML's Brian Yamada told the publication that until the industry agrees an AI cost structure, standard contract language will be hard to write.

Building makes sense if software is part of your business model. For most independents it is not. A custom stack needs engineers, connectors that break whenever a platform changes its API, and someone on call when they do. The holding networks have spent millions on exactly this. An independent that tries to match them in-house usually ends up with a fragile copy and a very tired head of digital.

Buying an integrated platform is the shortcut. That is the gap the Defy platform was built to close: the same analytical and AI capability as the holding networks, without the build cost or the maintenance burden. Whatever you choose, apply the test in the table above. Connected beats clever.

How do I prove the stack is worth it to clients?

If only a third of marketers are happy with what their martech delivers, an agency that can show value from its tools has an edge. Showing value means reporting outcomes, not activity.

  • Show the time you returned. If automation saves a day on every monthly report, say where that day went: a sharper competitor read, a test plan, a better brief.
  • Show the evidence. Clients trust AI conclusions they can check. Link every finding to the posts, ads or data behind it.
  • Show the decisions. The strongest proof is a decision the client made faster, or with more confidence, because of something your stack surfaced.

Tools are a cost line. A connected system that changes decisions is a reason to keep the agency.

The insight
Tools alone can't compose a masterpiece; harmony is born from their unison.

Frequently asked questions

What is an AI marketing stack for an agency?

An agency AI marketing stack is the set of connected tools that gather client, competitor and audience data, turn it into insight and automate delivery. The important word is connected: a stack shares one source of truth and passes work between tools without manual exports. A collection of separate apps, each with its own login and its own data, is not a stack, however clever each app is.

How many AI tools does an agency need?

Fewer than most own. Start from workflows rather than tool categories: an intelligence layer for client and category data, an interface where your team can question that data, and automation for the two or three delivery chores that eat the most hours. Each extra tool adds connections to maintain, so every addition should remove a named chore or a manual handoff.

Do I need to change client contracts when I use AI tools?

Probably, at least in part. Digiday reports that agencies are adding AI terms to existing master service agreements rather than rewriting them, usually when a client wants a seat in the agency's AI tool, asks for AI language, or when a new AI tool is used on their project. Clients increasingly ask about data use, human oversight, tool disclosure and ownership of outputs.

Should a small agency build its own AI tools?

Only if building software is part of the business model. Custom tools need engineers, connectors and constant maintenance as models and platform APIs change, and that burden usually lands on one or two people who were hired to do client work. Most independents get further by buying an integrated platform and spending their effort on the workflows and judgement that set them apart.
