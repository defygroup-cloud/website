Independent agency vs holding company: how independents compete on data

WPP is cutting 1,000 more roles and Omnicom has moved its AI platform team to a contractor. What the holdco data moat looks like now, and how independents compete.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 7 min read Also available in: English中文
The question behind this article

“As an independent agency, how can I use data as effectively as a holding company, and what data set-up do I actually need?”

Short answerHolding companies still lead on scale: proprietary platforms, media buying data and large specialist teams. But as network-grade analytics and AI become available on subscription, independents can match much of that capability without the build or maintenance cost. The deciding factor is shifting from who owns the data stack to who connects data to everyday client decisions fastest.

Key takeaways

  • Holding companies still lead on buying scale and proprietary data, but much of their analytics and AI capability can now be rented by independents.
  • Gartner's Jay Wilson told The Drum that agency fees have fallen to 19.2% of marketing budgets and predicts 15% by 2030.
  • Omnicom moved at least 468 staff behind its Omni AI platform to Endava, a sign that even the largest groups are rethinking who builds and maintains their technology.
  • The digital divide between agencies has shifted from access to tools towards skill in using them and turning that use into outcomes.
  • Independents should build data capability around client decisions, not architecture, and rent the category layer rather than build it.

Can an independent agency compete with a holding company on data? Yes, and the gap is closing faster than most network credentials decks admit. The advantage used to be owning expensive platforms and the teams to run them. Now that network-grade analytics and AI can be rented, the advantage is shifting to whoever connects data to client decisions fastest.

This year's news from the networks makes the point. The Drum, following a Financial Times report, says WPP is set to cut up to 1,000 more roles by year-end, with headcount at 97,388 at the end of June, down 6.4% on a year earlier. And Adweek reports that Omnicom moved at least 468 staffers, mostly the product and engineering talent behind its Omni AI platform, to IT services firm Endava in June and July, even as it pitches Omni as the keystone of its client offering after the IPG deal.

Do holding companies still have a data advantage?

In some areas, clearly. Scale buys proprietary data partnerships, media buying power and specialist teams that most independents cannot match. If the client's question is how to buy a nine-figure media budget across forty markets, a network's machinery still matters.

But the data moat looks different up close. A platform is only a moat if you can afford to keep it filled. Omnicom's move does not mean Omni is failing. It does show that even the largest groups are rethinking who should build and maintain their technology. That is a question independents answered years ago, mostly out of necessity. They rent.

What are the holdco cuts really telling independents?

Follow the fees. Gartner's Jay Wilson told The Drum that agency fees have fallen to 19.2% of marketing budgets, and he expects them to reach 15% by 2030. A shrinking fee pool leaves less room for large, labour-heavy structures.

Wilson's reading of who benefits is blunt. He told The Drum that AI is helping to scale the capabilities of independent agencies and making them viable direct competitors. He also pointed to Gartner research finding that most surveyed holdco agencies had reduced staff over the past year, while several independents had increased hiring.

None of that means the networks are finished. They are restructuring, and restructured networks can be formidable. It does mean the old pitch line, "only we have the data stack", is getting harder to defend. When intelligence becomes something you can subscribe to, the question becomes who uses it best.

Why is the digital divide between agencies moving?

Sociologists use Digital Divide Theory to describe the gap between people who have access to technology and those who do not. The more useful part of the idea is what happens next. When access gets cheap, the divide does not disappear. It moves, first to who has the skills to use the technology, then to who turns that use into real outcomes.

Agencies are living through exactly that shift. Not long ago the divide was access: networks had data platforms and engineers, independents had spreadsheets and goodwill. Today a twenty-person shop can subscribe to competitor ad tracking, social listening and AI analysis that once needed a dedicated team. The new divide is usage. Who asks the better questions? Who wires data into Tuesday's decisions rather than a quarterly deck?

This is where the insight earns its place: innovation thrives in connection, not competition. Independents will not win by building a smaller copy of a holdco platform. They win by connecting. That means plugging into shared intelligence rather than rebuilding it, joining client data to market data and putting the answers in front of the people who make decisions. A network has to coordinate thousands of people to do that. An independent needs a good stack and a short corridor.

Independent agency vs holding company: how do they compare on data?

DimensionHolding companyIndependent agency with a rented stack
PlatformProprietary, built and branded in-houseLicensed from specialist platforms
Cost modelLarge fixed engineering and maintenance costsSubscriptions that scale with the client base
Speed of changeSlower, as changes ripple across many agencies and marketsFaster, with tools and workflows swapped in weeks
Breadth of dataWide, including scale media and partnership dataStrong on market, social and competitor data; thinner on proprietary buying data
Who uses itOften specialist analytics teamsIdeally everyone, from planners to account leads
Main riskCost pressure on the teams behind the platformVendor dependence and patchy adoption

Read the table as a set of trade-offs, not a scoreboard. The network's edge is breadth and buying scale. The independent's edge is speed and proximity. For most clients, the row that matters most is "who uses it". Data that sits with a specialist team informs a quarterly review. Data that every planner can question informs this week's work.

How should an independent agency build its data capability?

Start with decisions, not architecture. Here is a practical sequence for the first 90 days.

  1. List the decisions clients pay you to influence. Audience choice, message, channel mix, creative selection, budget shifts. Five is plenty.
  2. Map the data behind each decision. Some sits with the client (CRM, sales, site analytics), some is public category data (competitor ads, social conversation) and some is your own (past campaign results).
  3. Rent the category layer. Do not build scrapers or dashboards for public data. Subscribe to a platform that already covers every client and category you work in.
  4. Put the questions where people already work. If your team can interrogate data in plain language inside an assistant such as Claude, they will use it. Yet another dashboard tends to be forgotten by March.
  5. Make traceability a rule. No AI conclusion goes to a client without the posts, ads or figures behind it.
  6. Productise one output. A monthly competitor brief or a pre-pitch category scan, priced and delivered consistently.
  7. Review at day 90. Keep what got used, drop what got ignored and choose the next decision to improve.

This is the logic behind the Defy platform: the analytical and AI capability of a holding network, delivered as one stack an independent can run for its clients without building or maintaining it.

Where should independents still be careful?

Renting a stack does not make you a data agency. Three cautions.

  • Client data access. The most valuable data usually belongs to the client. Agree access, governance and privacy rules early, and in writing.
  • Specialist depth. Econometrics, incrementality testing and large-scale media optimisation still need specialist skills. Partner rather than pretend.
  • Adoption. A tool only the head of insight opens is a cost, not a capability. Train the account team, not just the analysts.

The holdcos are not standing still. They are reorganising around AI at enormous scale. Independents do not need to out-build them. They need to out-connect them.

The insight
Innovation thrives in connection, not competition.

Digital Divide Theory says that when technology becomes cheap, the gap moves from access to use. Independents win by connecting shared intelligence to client decisions, not by building a smaller copy of a network platform.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an independent agency and a holding company agency?

A holding company agency belongs to a large group, such as WPP, Omnicom or Dentsu, that owns many agency brands and shares central resources like data platforms and media buying. An independent agency is not part of such a group. It typically has less central infrastructure, fewer layers of approval and more freedom to choose its own tools and partners.

Can independent agencies access the same data as holding companies?

Not all of it. Networks still hold proprietary data from media buying at scale and from large partnership deals. But much of the analytical work, including competitor ad tracking, social listening, audience clustering and AI analysis, is now available on subscription. For most strategic and creative decisions, that rented capability covers the data an independent actually needs.

Should an agency build its own data platform or buy one?

For most independents, buy. Building means hiring engineers, maintaining data pipelines and keeping pace with every platform change, a fixed cost that does not shrink when a client leaves. Omnicom's transfer of its Omni platform staff to a contractor shows even the largest groups are rethinking that burden. Build only where your data or method is genuinely unique.

What data architecture does an independent agency need?

A simple one. Three layers cover most needs: client-owned data such as CRM and sales, market data such as competitor ads and social conversation, and an interpretation layer where your team can question both in plain language. Agree access rules for client data early, rent the market layer and judge the whole set-up by whether it changes client decisions.
