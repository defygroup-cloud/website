Audience research tools: what planners should automate first

WARC says fear of AI replacing strategists jumped 15 points this year. Here is what planners should automate first, and why freed time is where new thinking comes from.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 7 min read

The question behind this article

“Which audience research tools should I use, and which parts of my strategy work should I automate first?”

Short answerAutomate the gathering, not the thinking. Planners should hand audience research tools the high-frequency, low-judgement work first: competitor ad audits, social listening round-ups and first-draft personas built from real audience content. Insist that every AI conclusion links back to its source posts. Then reinvest the saved hours in what clients pay strategists for: judgement, sharper questions and the creative leap.

Key takeaways

  • WARC's Future of Strategy 2026 found 56% of strategists see AI replacing strategy roles as their top expected threat, up 15 points, while 66% remain optimistic.
  • The first tasks to automate are high-frequency and low-judgement: competitor audits, social listening round-ups and draft personas.
  • Desk research is a small market failure inside agencies: unpriced, unmeasured and paid for with senior planners' judgement.
  • A good audience research tool shows the real posts behind every conclusion, so the evidence can go in front of a client.
  • Automation only produces new thinking if the agency decides in advance where the saved hours will go.

Which audience research tools should a planner automate first? Start with the work clients never see and rarely value: competitor audits, social listening round-ups and first-draft personas. Automate the gathering, keep the thinking. Planners who get that split right end up with more time for the part of strategy nobody can buy off the shelf.

The timing is not academic. WARC's Future of Strategy 2026, its annual survey of mostly agency-side strategists, names AI adoption as the biggest change to the strategy role. 56% of strategists now say AI replacing strategy roles is their top expected threat, up 15 points in a year. Yet 66% still see clear reasons for optimism. Both numbers are right, and the gap between them is what this piece is about.

Why are strategists so worried about AI?

Because a lot of what gets billed as strategy is really assembly. Pulling screenshots of competitor ads. Reading 400 comments to find the three that matter. Building a persona slide from a survey that was out of date before the brief landed. If that is what you think the job is, the fear is rational. Machines are very good at assembly.

WARC's own framing points somewhere more useful. The report places the strategist's value in judgement, in knowing which questions are worth asking and in sensing what people might want next. None of that is assembly. The threat is real for the planner who only compiles. For the one who interprets, it looks more like a promotion.

Why does desk research swallow so much of a planner's week?

Economists use the term market failure for a situation where a market, left to itself, puts resources in the wrong place. Nobody is being stupid. The incentives simply point the wrong way.

Agency strategy has a small, chronic version of this. Clients buy strategy as a fee, usually folded into a retainer or a project. Nobody pays a line item for desk research, so it is never priced, never measured and never questioned. It just expands to fill the evenings. Meanwhile the scarcest resource in the building, a senior planner's judgement, gets spent on copying and pasting.

The result is a system that under-supplies the thing it values most. Clients get decks that are thick with data and thin on a point of view. Planners burn out doing work that does not use their best skills. Everyone loses a little, every week, and nobody sends an invoice for it.

That is why efficiency is not the dull part of the AI story. It is the correction. When the gathering takes minutes instead of days, the hours go back to the work clients actually pay for: the tension worth building on, the proposition, the leap. Efficiency is the mother of innovation, not because faster is better, but because freed time is the only raw material new thinking has.

What should planners automate first?

Rank every recurring task on two tests. How often does it come round? And how much judgement does it genuinely need? High-frequency, low-judgement work goes first. Here is a starting order for a typical agency planning team.

How do you choose audience research tools without buying another dashboard?

Most planning teams already keep a graveyard of logins. Here is a practical way to pick a tool that gets used rather than admired.

  1. Log two weeks of planner time. Split it into gathering, analysing and thinking. You need your own baseline, not a vendor's.
  2. Pick one recurring deliverable. The monthly competitor report or the pre-pitch category scan are good candidates. Automate that end to end before touching anything else.
  3. Prefer real behaviour to stated intent. Tools that read what audiences and competitors actually publish show you what people do. Use surveys to test hypotheses, not to generate them.
  4. Demand traceability. Every AI conclusion should click through to the posts or ads behind it. If you cannot show a client the evidence, it does not go in the deck.
  5. Test it on a category you know cold. Run the tool on a client you have worked with for years. When it tells you something you know is wrong, you have learnt how far to trust it.
  6. Put it where the team already works. A tool your planners can question in plain language, inside an assistant they already use, beats a dashboard that needs a training day.
  7. Decide in advance where the saved hours go. If you do not, they will vanish into more meetings.

What stays human in strategy?

Judgement, for a start. AI can cluster a million posts into themes. It cannot tell you which theme a cautious CMO will defend in front of her board. It can draft personas from real audience content. It cannot sit in a focus group and notice the silence after a question.

Then there is taste. Strategy is mostly choosing what to leave out. Machines are generous by default and give you everything. The planner's job is to throw most of it away and defend what remains.

And there is the client conversation. The best strategic insight is often the one the client half-knew and needed someone to say out loud. That takes trust, context and nerve. None of those come with a subscription.

What does this mean for planning teams and juniors?

The awkward question is how juniors learn if desk research disappears. Desk research was the apprenticeship. It taught people what a category looks like from the inside.

The answer is to redesign the apprenticeship, not to protect the drudgery. Give juniors the automated output and make their job interrogating it: what is missing, what looks wrong, what is surprising. That builds judgement faster than formatting screenshots ever did. It is the split we designed the Defy platform around: machines gather and sort, people question and decide.

WARC's optimists and pessimists are looking at the same machine. The difference is what they plan to do with the time it hands back.

The insight
Efficiency is the mother of innovation.

In economic terms, unpriced desk research is a market failure: it quietly consumes the planner judgement clients actually pay for. Automating the gathering moves that scarce resource back to thinking.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best audience research tool for an agency?

There is no single best tool, but there is a reliable test. Choose one that reads real published content from audiences and competitors, covers every category you work in, shows the source posts behind each conclusion and fits into tools your team already uses. Pilot it on one recurring deliverable, such as a monthly competitor report, before rolling it out across the agency.

Will AI replace strategists and planners?

AI is taking over parts of the job, chiefly gathering, sorting and summarising. It is far weaker at choosing which problem to solve, reading a client's appetite for risk and making the creative leap. WARC's Future of Strategy 2026 places strategic value in exactly those areas. Planners who interpret rather than compile are likely to become more valuable, not less.

How much time can automating audience research save?

It depends on how your team works today, and anyone quoting a universal figure is guessing. The dependable approach is to log two weeks of planner time, split into gathering, analysing and thinking, then automate one recurring deliverable and log again. The difference is your number, and it will persuade a finance director far more than a vendor benchmark.

Is AI-generated audience research reliable enough to show clients?

Only if you can trace it. Treat any AI conclusion as a hypothesis until you have seen the posts, ads or comments it came from. Test the tool on a category you know well to learn where it goes wrong. Then present findings with the evidence attached, exactly as you would with any other research partner.
