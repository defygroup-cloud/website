Agency burnout: fewer repetitive tasks, more time for creativity

Not all agency stress is equal. How to tell the pressure that gives the craft its meaning from the repetitive load that burns teams out, and which part you can automate.

The question behind this article

“I work at an agency and I'm constantly stressed: how do I deal with it before I burn out?”

Short answerManaging agency stress starts with separating two loads: creative work, which is demanding but meaningful, and repetitive tasks such as reports, timesheets and format resizes. The second can be reduced or automated. Doing so frees up time and energy for the first and tackles one of the root causes of burnout. If the exhaustion persists, seek professional help.

Key takeaways

  • Agency burnout doesn't come only from the intensity of creative work but from the repetitive load that competes with it: reports, timesheets, resizes and status meetings.
  • According to Campaign, agencies such as X&O and Beers With Friends work in short sprints with a single client, a model that puts focus ahead of fragmentation.
  • Burnout theory describes three dimensions (exhaustion, cynicism and inefficacy), and mechanical work feeds all three.
  • Automation only reduces stress if the time it frees up is protected for judgement-led work or rest, not filled with more load.
  • If exhaustion persists or affects your health, the answer is to talk to a professional, not to rearrange your diary.

How do you manage agency stress before it turns into burnout? First, by separating two kinds of pressure. There's the kind that gives the craft its meaning: defending an idea, winning a pitch, landing a difficult campaign. And there's the kind that simply grinds you down: the monthly report rebuilt by hand, Friday's timesheets, the tenth resize of the same banner. The second kind can be reduced, and a good chunk of it can be automated. That won't fix everything, but it goes after the most avoidable cause.

The subject is back on the table. In September, Campaign ran a feature on the agency sprint model: teams that throw themselves at a single client for short bursts, such as the exactly five days Beers With Friends dedicates to the work. Eric Segal, co-founder of X&O, calls it "a constant hustle" that "keeps us honest, hungry". His co-founder, Brett Banker, sums up what many people were saying about the traditional model: teams "spread across too much", juggling everything at once.

Why is burnout so common in advertising agencies?

Because the agency model mixes three volatile ingredients: deadlines set by someone else, clients who change their minds, and a culture that for decades has mistaken being busy for being valuable.

There's a fourth ingredient that gets talked about far less: admin. Anyone who has worked in an agency knows the list. Timesheets, monthly reports, status meetings, budget tracking, resource planning, manual resizing, competitor audits, invoices that need chasing. Nobody goes into advertising for any of these, and every one of them competes for the same hours as the creative work.

The result is depressingly familiar: days that stretch into the evening so you can finally do "the real work" once the inbox goes quiet.

What the sprint model gives away

Campaign's feature isn't about mental health, and it would be unfair to credit it with conclusions it doesn't draw. But it leaves an interesting clue. Banker's diagnosis points not to intensity but to fragmentation: people spread across too many fronts at once.

A sprint is intense, but it has a focus and an end. The typical agency day is the opposite: ten workstreams open, none of them closed, and an admin queue that never empties. For many people, what wears them out isn't working hard on something that matters. It's working hard on things that don't.

That said, let's not romanticise the hustle. A frantic pace can be exhilarating for five days. As a way of life, it takes its toll.

What does burnout theory say about creative work?

The psychologist Christina Maslach, a leading figure in burnout research, describes it in three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism (a gradual detachment from the work and the people around it) and a sense of inefficacy.

Repetitive work feeds all three. It exhausts through sheer volume. It breeds cynicism because nobody seems to care about it: think of the forty-slide report the client barely flicks through. And it produces inefficacy because the day ends without you having done the thing you were hired to do: think.

Creativity needs exactly the opposite: uninterrupted time, energy in the tank and a sense that your work counts. Creativity flourishes in the soil of self-care, and in an agency that self-care isn't only a personal matter. It also depends on how the work is designed.

Not all of them, and it pays to be honest about that. This table separates what a machine can take off your plate from what needs a different kind of fix:

How to lower the pressure in five steps (without waiting for the agency to change)

If you run a team, apply this to the whole team. If you don't, start with your own week:

  1. Log a real week. Note down, in half-hour blocks, what you do and how it leaves you: with more energy or with less.
  2. Sort it. Separate judgement work (strategy, ideas, client relationships) from mechanical work (copying data, reformatting, chasing approvals).
  3. Pick the two mechanical tasks that eat the most hours and take them to your line manager with numbers: how many hours a month they consume and what you'd do with that time instead.
  4. Automate or simplify one of them first. A template, a reporting agent or an automated monitoring set-up. Measure before and after.
  5. Protect the time you win back. Block it out in your calendar for deep work or for rest. If you don't protect it, it will fill itself, and not with better things.

What agency leaders need to do

Individual stress has a structural side that no breathing exercise will fix. The big holding-company networks have spent years, and millions, investing in data, analytics and AI; many independent agencies are still doing by hand what others automated long ago.

The good news is that this capability no longer requires an in-house development team. The Defy platform, for example, includes Defy Agents, AI workflows that handle the repetitive delivery so people can focus on the work that wins clients.

One warning, though: automation can't become an excuse to pile on more work. If the hours you free up are simply filled with another client and the team stays the same size, burnout doesn't disappear. It just changes shape.

When reorganising the work isn't enough

Everything above reduces one source of stress, but it's no substitute for care. If the exhaustion has lasted for weeks, is affecting your sleep or your health, or you feel you simply can't go on, it isn't a productivity problem. Talk to a health professional and, if you can, to your manager or HR.

Asking for help in time is also a matter of professional judgement. Probably the most important kind.

Frequently asked questions

What are the signs of burnout in an agency?

The three classic signs are exhaustion that a weekend doesn't fix, cynicism towards the work or the clients, and a feeling that nothing you do makes any difference. In agencies it often shows up in brilliant people who stop putting forward ideas, do the bare minimum or talk about clients with contempt. Spotting it early makes all the difference.

Does automating tasks really reduce stress?

It reduces one of its sources: the volume of mechanical work that adds no meaning. But the effect depends on what happens to the time that's freed up. Protect it for judgement-led work or rest and it brings relief; fill it with more work and it doesn't.

What can I do if my agency won't invest in tools?

Start by measuring. Log for a week how many hours you spend on mechanical tasks and work out their rough monthly cost. Sometimes templates, clear rules on client amends or fewer status meetings are enough. With numbers in hand, the conversation with leadership stops being a complaint and becomes a business case.

When should I seek professional help?

When the exhaustion lasts for weeks, affects your sleep, appetite or health, or you experience intense anxiety or a feeling that you can't go on. At that point it's no longer an organisational problem: talk to a doctor or a mental health professional and, if you can, to HR. Reorganising your work helps, but it doesn't replace that support.
