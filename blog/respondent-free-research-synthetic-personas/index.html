Research without respondents: are synthetic personas promise or mirage?

Research without human respondents is arriving in agencies. Useful for exploring, risky for concluding: how to use it without telling yourself stories.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 6 min read Also available in: English中文Français
The question behind this article

“Can I trust synthetic personas to understand Gen Z or older consumers, or do I still need real respondents?”

Short answerFor exploring, yes; for concluding, no. Synthetic personas test scenarios and messages quickly, but they play back what the model has learned, generational clichés included, and claim no representativeness. For generational research, anchor every conclusion in real observed behaviour, such as the content these audiences actually publish, then use synthetic personas to test.

Key takeaways

  • Respondent-free research platforms, such as Limpid, launched by Patrick van Bloeme, let you run a qualitative study on synthetic personas in a few hours, without claiming statistical representativeness.
  • The main risk is the data illusion effect: a fluent, precise answer inspires confidence even when it rests on no real behaviour.
  • Generational research is particularly exposed, because a model learns the clichés about a generation as much as its behaviour.
  • Good practice is to anchor insights in content that has actually been published, then use synthetic personas to explore and test.
  • Every recommendation should state what comes from synthetic, observed and self-reported sources.

Can you understand Gen Z, young parents or the soon-to-retire without asking a single human being? Short answer: with synthetic personas you can explore, test and rough out ideas very quickly, but you can't conclude. For generational research, synthetic is an accelerator. It doesn't replace observing real behaviour.

This is no longer a theoretical question. Stratégies, the French trade title, reports how Patrick van Bloeme, co-founder of Novatris, which became Harris Interactive and then Toluna, has launched Limpid, a platform for qualitative research without human respondents. Companies can use it to run a complete study on synthetic personas, "from brief to deliverables", in a few hours and autonomously. When someone who spent thirty years running research institutes makes that bet, agencies would do well to take a close look.

What does respondent-free research promise?

According to Stratégies, Limpid lets you study behaviours, usage and reactions to concepts, messages or situations. You can simulate a sudden price rise, a new health crisis or life moments such as moving house or retiring.

The platform also sets out its own limits, which is to its credit: it doesn't claim statistical representativeness and doesn't produce quantitative surveys. In other words, it is a tool for testing scenarios quickly, not for measuring a market.

For an agency, the appeal is obvious. A pitch brief lands on Monday, the recommendation is due on Friday: there is no time for classic qualitative fieldwork to happen. Synthetic fills the gap.

Why does a fluent answer look so much like the truth?

This is where the trap snaps shut. A synthetic persona answers in impeccable, coherent, nuanced language. It has a first name, an age, habits. All of it looks like knowledge.

Call it the data illusion effect: the more precise and polished a piece of information looks, the more credit we give it, regardless of how solid it actually is. Synthetic research pushes that effect to the maximum, because the form is perfect by design.

Yet a synthetic persona lives through nothing. It plays back regularities learned from vast amounts of text. Data reveals patterns, but it cannot define humanity. A statistical pattern about "older people" is not a 67-year-old woman hesitating over whether to switch banks.

Is generational research a minefield?

Yes, for a simple reason: generations are already stereotypes. "Gen Z" doesn't exist as a homogeneous bloc. A student in Lyon, an apprentice in rural France and a young Parisian executive have little in common beyond their year of birth.

A language model also learns, and learns a great deal, from what is said about generations: articles, opinion pieces, studies, debates. So it risks knowing the discourse about Gen Z better than the way young people actually express themselves. The result: a synthetic persona can make the cliché more convincing instead of correcting it.

Imagine an agency preparing a campaign for a health insurer targeting the newly retired. The synthetic panel confirms what everyone already believes: caution, a need for reassurance, wariness of all things digital. Meanwhile, the real newly retired are posting about their travels, their volunteering projects and their grandchildren. The reassuring campaign falls flat.

No method wins on every row. The right question is not "which one should I choose?" but "in what order should I combine them?".

How do you combine synthetic and real without kidding yourself?

  1. Start with what is observed. Analyse what the target generation actually publishes in your category: topics, words, tensions, formats. That is your foundation.
  2. Build personas from that content. Group posts by profile and by concern, rather than starting from an age bracket.
  3. Use synthetic to explore. Put scenarios, concepts or hooks to synthetic personas to prune the weak routes quickly.
  4. Test every conclusion against reality. Every claim that comes out of synthetic research must find an echo in real posts. No echo, no insight.
  5. Measure when you need to measure. If the decision commits a significant budget, validate it with a representative sample or a real-world test.
  6. Document the method in the recommendation. Tell the client what comes from synthetic, observed and self-reported sources. They will thank you the day the question comes up in the boardroom.

What advertisers are going to ask you

As synthetic research spreads, one question will come up in every presentation: "Where does this insight come from?" An agency that answers "from a panel of AI-generated personas" will have to say a lot more. An agency that can show the real posts behind each conclusion won't have that problem.

Synthetic is a good tool, used in its proper place. The danger isn't the machine. It is the temptation to believe that a well-written portrait is worth as much as an observation.

Frequently asked questions

What is research without human respondents?

It is research carried out on AI-generated synthetic personas instead of real people. According to Stratégies, the Limpid platform lets you run a complete qualitative study this way, from brief to deliverables, in a few hours. It is designed for testing scenarios, concepts or messages, but it claims no statistical representativeness and does not produce quantitative surveys.

Can synthetic personas replace a panel?

No, and serious players don't claim they can. A representative panel is for measuring and sizing; a synthetic persona is for exploring quickly. The two answer different questions. For a decision that commits a significant budget, validation on a real sample or a real-world test is still necessary.

How do you get reliable generational insights quickly?

Start from the content the target generation actually publishes in your category, group it into profiles by concern rather than by age, then identify the recurring tensions. Then use synthetic personas to test routes. That two-step approach gives you the speed of synthetic research without losing the anchor in observable behaviour.

What is the difference between a synthetic persona and a persona built from social listening?

A synthetic persona is simulated by a language model from what it has learned. A social listening persona is built by clustering an audience's real posts. The first is plausible; the second is observable and verifiable, because you can trace it back to the content it rests on.
