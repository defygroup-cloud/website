Media consumption data in Singapore: what to trust as networks shrink

Dentsu is cutting overseas entities and APAC is contracting. Here is how to find Singapore media consumption data you can trust, and how to read it without fooling yourself.

Defy Editorial Published 16 October 2026 4 min read Also available in: English中文
The question behind this article

“Where can I find accurate, reliable data on how people in Singapore consume media?”

Short answerNo single source gives you accurate media consumption data for Singapore. Use official statistics and regulator surveys for broad reach, audience measurement panels and platform data for exposure, and real published content for what people actually engage with and talk about. Compare them, note each one's blind spot, and treat precise averages with suspicion.

Key takeaways

  • Accurate media consumption data for Singapore comes from combining sources, because each one measures something different and misses something important.
  • Surveys, platform-reported figures and measurement panels often disagree, and the disagreement is information rather than an error to average away.
  • Dentsu named Singapore among the APAC markets that contracted in the first half of 2026 as it cuts overseas entities, a good moment for independents to own their market intelligence.
  • Precise numbers create an illusion of understanding: an average viewing figure says nothing about why different audiences choose what they watch.
  • Real published posts show what audiences actually engage with and say, and conclusions drawn from them can be checked against the source.

Where can you find accurate media consumption data for Singapore? Not in one place. Official statistics and regulator surveys give you broad reach and device use. Audience measurement panels and platform dashboards give you exposure, each with its own bias. Real published content shows what people actually engage with and talk about. Accuracy comes from comparing them, not from finding the one perfect table.

The question matters more this year, because the big networks that used to hand clients a polished market deck are rewiring their Asian businesses. Campaign Asia-Pacific reported in August that Dentsu plans 70 to 80 entity reductions in 2026, and possibly another 50 to 80 by 2028, on top of a job-cutting programme that is now 88% complete. Singapore was named among the APAC markets that contracted in the first half. And this week Marketing-Interactive reported that ADK and Stagwell have signed a three-year master agreement that leans on Stagwell's data and analytics.

Why is reliable media consumption data in Singapore so hard to pin down?

Because every source measures something slightly different, and most were built to answer somebody else's question.

  • Surveys ask people what they do. People answer honestly and are still unreliable, because memory is a poor stopwatch.
  • Platform figures come from the companies selling the inventory. They are precise, and they mark their own homework.
  • Measurement panels are rigorous about what they track and blind to what they do not.
  • Agency market decks blend all three, often without saying which number came from where.

Singapore adds its own complications: a multilingual, multi-generational audience that moves between local, regional and global platforms in a single evening. Any single average flattens all of that.

What do the network shake-ups mean for independents in APAC?

Dentsu's half-year update describes APAC excluding Japan as its "next growth base", while the region remained loss-making in the first half, with organic revenue down 3.8%. Australia, China, Singapore, Taiwan and Thailand contracted; India was the only major market to grow, according to Campaign Asia-Pacific. The group says it will rationalise its APAC portfolio and focus on data, social and commerce. It has not said which businesses will be affected.

Read that alongside the ADK and Stagwell deal and a pattern emerges. Networks are concentrating: fewer entities, more alliances, and data and analytics as the asset each side brings to the table. Stagwell Asia Pacific's managing director, Randy Duax, summed up the partnership: "We've built the foundation. Now we're ready to scale."

For independent agencies and in-house teams in Singapore, the lesson is not that the networks are leaving. It is that market intelligence is increasingly sold as part of an integrated network package. If you want it on your own terms, you need to build it yourself.

Why can precise numbers mislead you?

Call it the data illusion effect: the more precise a number looks, the more we trust it to explain behaviour it was never designed to explain. Picture a slide that gives Singapore's average daily video time to the minute. It feels like knowledge. It tells you nothing about who watches what, in which language, on which screen, or why.

The danger is rarely bad data. It is good data asked the wrong question. A strategist who builds a plan on an average has planned for a person who does not exist.

Data reveals patterns, but it cannot define humanity. The numbers can tell you that something is happening. Only people, and what they choose to publish, react to and argue about, can tell you why.

How to build a Singapore market-intelligence pack without a network

  1. Write down the decision first. Channel mix, creative language, launch timing. The decision tells you which data matters.
  2. Set a baseline from official sources. Use government and regulator surveys for reach and device use, and note the fieldwork date next to every figure.
  3. Layer measured and platform data. Record who produced each figure and how. Never blend them into one number without saying so.
  4. Add real published content. Track what audiences and competitors in the category actually post and respond to, across languages and platforms.
  5. Look for disagreement. Where surveys and platform data diverge, find out why. That is usually where the insight is hiding.
  6. Trace every claim to its source. If a conclusion cannot be traced back to data or real posts, it does not go in the client deck.
  7. Refresh monthly, not annually. A market deck that is a year old describes a market that has already moved on.

Where does real published content fit in?

It fills the gap the other sources leave: the why. Surveys and panels count. Published posts show what people care about, in their own words and languages, and what competitors are actually saying to them.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one official source for media consumption in Singapore?

No single source covers everything. Official statistics and regulator surveys are good for population-level device ownership and broad usage, but they are self-reported and published with a lag. For channel planning, combine them with audience measurement, platform data and real published content, and record where every figure came from.

Why do platform figures and surveys disagree?

They measure different things. Surveys record what people remember and report, while platforms count activity on their own services and estimate reach with their own methods. Neither is wrong by default. A gap between them often points to something worth investigating, such as habits people under-report or audiences a platform overestimates.

Is Dentsu leaving Singapore?

Dentsu has not said that. Campaign Asia-Pacific reported that the group plans 70 to 80 entity reductions in 2026 and possibly 50 to 80 more by 2028, and named Singapore among the APAC markets that contracted in the first half of 2026. Dentsu said it will rationalise its APAC portfolio but did not identify which businesses would be affected.

How can a small agency get market intelligence without a big research budget?

Combine free official statistics with the platform data you already have access to, then add analysis of real published content from audiences and competitors in the category. Keep a simple source log so every number in a client deck can be traced. Refresh it monthly. Consistent, traceable evidence often beats an expensive annual report that is out of date when it lands.
