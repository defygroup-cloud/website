AI adoption in agencies: document the real workflow before you automate

AI on a bad process just gives you a fast bad process. Before your agency automates anything, watch how the work really moves and find the one bottleneck worth fixing.

The question behind this article

“How do I integrate AI tools into my agency's existing workflows without making things worse?”

Short answerStart with the work, not the tool. Sit with the people who actually do the job, map how it really moves rather than what the SOP says, and find the single bottleneck: the brief, production, approval, the handover or a decision. If you can't name it, don't automate anything yet. AI applied to a broken process just gives you a faster broken process.

Key takeaways

  • AI applied to a bad process gives you a fast bad process, because automation multiplies whatever it touches, including confusion.
  • An SOP shows how work should happen; a workflow shows how it really happens, and the gap between the two is where bottlenecks hide.
  • Agency bottlenecks usually sit in one of five places: the brief, production, approval, the handover or decision-making.
  • If you cannot name the bottleneck, do not automate anything; fix the human part first, then pilot AI on the repetitive steps that remain.

How do you integrate AI tools into an agency's existing workflows? Not by starting with the tools. Start by watching how the work really gets done, find the one place where it gets stuck, and only then decide whether AI belongs there. Apply AI to a bad process and you simply get a fast bad process.

That was the uncomfortable lesson from an agency founder speaking at an AI adoption session in Singapore this week. He spent 18 months trying to make his roughly 20-person agency "AI-native" and was candid about what he would undo. His most memorable image: put a robot vacuum in a messy house and it doesn't clean anything. It drags the mess across the floor.

The timing is pointed. Digiday reports that agencies are patching AI into their existing master service agreements clause by clause rather than rewriting them. The triggers are a client wanting a seat in the agency's proprietary AI tool, a client asking for AI language, or a new AI tool turning up on a client project. Mile Marker's CEO told Digiday: "We are at the point now where we are just simply working that into existing MSAs." Contracts get patched. So do workflows. Patches on patches is how agencies end up automating chaos.

Why does AI on a bad process give you a fast bad process?

Because automation multiplies whatever it touches. If the brief arrives half-formed, an AI drafting tool produces half-formed concepts faster. If approval sits with one overbooked client services director, faster production just builds a longer queue outside her door.

Most agencies know their processes are messy. What they underestimate is how much of the mess is load-bearing. The Singapore founder compared his business to his own untidy home: chaos to a visitor, but he knew exactly where his wallet was. Agencies run the same way. How a pitch really comes together, or why one client's reports always need a second pass, lives in people's heads.

That knowledge is invisible to an AI tool. It is often invisible to new hires, too. His argument was that the first job is to make it accessible to both, before anyone buys another seat.

What is the difference between an SOP and a real workflow?

An SOP describes how the work is supposed to happen. A workflow is how it actually happens. The founder's advice was blunt: sit next to the subject-matter expert and watch them do the job. The gap between the SOP and what they really do is where the bottlenecks hide.

As the diagram shows, the SOP is a tidy checklist. The real workflow loops back on itself, detours through Slack and email, waits for someone, and has one point where everything piles up. That point, marked in red, is the only place where automation will pay off.

Every agency has its own version. The project-management tool says creative review happens on Tuesdays; in reality the ECD reviews on her phone between pitches and the team works late on Thursdays. The media SOP says the monthly report is "pull data, build deck, send". In reality an analyst spends half a day reconciling platform numbers that never quite match.

Where do agency bottlenecks usually hide?

The talk named five usual suspects: production, approval, the brief, the handover and decision-making. The rule that came with them: if you cannot identify which one is your bottleneck, do not automate anything. Here is how they tend to show up across agency types.

Notice the order. In every row the fix is human first and technical second. AI rarely solves an unclear brief or an absent decision-maker. It just makes their cost visible faster.

Why does this keep happening?

Systems Theory offers a plain explanation. A system's output is shaped by how its parts connect, not by how good any single part is. Speed up one step and the whole only improves if that step was the constraint. If the constraint sits elsewhere, you have simply built a bigger pile in front of it.

An agency is a system of people, briefs, clients, tools and deadlines. Dropping a powerful AI tool into one corner of it is like fitting a faster engine to a car stuck in traffic. Impressive under the bonnet, no quicker to the meeting.

The contract patching Digiday describes is the same pattern at the commercial level: add a clause each time a new tool appears rather than redesign how the relationship works. It is understandable. It also raises the stakes. If clients start asking for seats in your proprietary AI tools, they will see the process underneath. A fast bad process is much harder to hide when the client is logged in.

How do I document a workflow before automating it?

  1. Pick one workflow that hurts. Choose something frequent and painful, such as the monthly client report, the competitor audit or the pitch deck. Not "everything".
  2. Find the person who really does it. Not the head of department who wrote the SOP, but the account executive or analyst who does it every week.
  3. Sit next to them for a full cycle. Watch the work from trigger to delivery. Note every tool, message, wait and workaround. Ask "why" more often than "what".
  4. Draw the real flow next to the SOP. Put the two side by side, as in the diagram, and mark every place they differ.
  5. Time the waits, not just the tasks. Agency work often moves quickly and then sits for days. The longest wait usually points to the bottleneck.
  6. Name the bottleneck in one word. Brief, production, approval, handover or decision. If the team can't agree, keep watching. Don't automate yet.
  7. Fix the human part, then test the AI part. Change the process first, then pilot automation on the steps that are still repetitive, and compare the result with the old way.

Write the result down somewhere both people and AI can read it. A documented workflow is the first asset an AI system can actually use, and the first thing a new hire will thank you for.

What should an agency automate first?

Once the bottleneck is clear, the best first candidates are usually the chores nobody went into advertising to do: monthly reports, competitor audits, manual resizing, status updates. They are repetitive, they follow rules you can now describe, and the judgement sits around them rather than inside them.

Frequently asked questions

Should we write SOPs before adopting AI in our agency?

SOPs help, but they are not enough. An SOP records how work should happen; AI needs to know how it actually happens. Watch the people who do the job, document the real workflow including the waits and workarounds, and keep it somewhere both your team and your AI tools can read. Then update the SOP so it matches reality.

What if we can't identify a bottleneck?

Then don't automate yet. Keep observing, time the waits between steps and ask the people doing the work where it gets stuck. Often the bottleneck is a person or a decision rather than a task, such as one overstretched approver or an unclear brief. Automating before you know where the constraint is usually just moves the pile-up somewhere else, faster.

How long does it take to map one agency workflow?

Long enough to watch at least one full cycle of the work from start to finish. For a weekly report that might be a week; for a monthly report or a pitch, a month or one live project. The goal is not a perfect diagram but an honest picture of where time disappears and who the work waits for.

Do AI clauses in client contracts change how we should automate?

They raise the stakes. Digiday reports that agencies are adding AI terms to existing master service agreements when a client wants a seat in a proprietary AI tool, asks for AI language, or when a new AI tool is used on a client project. If clients can see or use your AI workflows, the process underneath needs to be sound, documented and checked by a person.
