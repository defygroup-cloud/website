How to Increase Social Media Engagement: Predict It Before You Post

Most posts get ignored because they look like everything else and help no one. Here's how to close the gap between what you think works and what your audience wants.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20266 min readEnglish
“
The question behind this article

What can I do to make my social media posts more engaging and reach more people?

Short answerTo increase social media engagement, make each post useful enough that someone would save, share or act on it, and check that before you publish. Start from real customer questions, lead with a specific hook, show the product solving a problem, and end with one clear action. Then compare drafts before posting rather than guessing afterward.

Key takeaways

  • Most small-business posts get ignored because they look like everyone else's and give the viewer nothing to do with them.
  • Your audience knows what it wants and you only see the likes afterward; that information gap, more than the algorithm, is why posts fall flat.
  • Pinterest is urging brands to make content that helps people act and get back to their lives, citing Gen Z fatigue with endless scrolling.
  • Customer reviews, DMs, sales questions and comments are the cheapest audience research a small business has.
  • Predicting engagement before you publish, with a simple scorecard or a scoring tool, costs less than learning from a week of silence.

Why do my social media posts get ignored?

If your posts get a few likes from friends and little else, the algorithm is rarely the whole story. Usually the post looks like a hundred others and gives the viewer no reason to stop, save, reply or act. To increase social media engagement, make each post more useful, and check that usefulness before you publish instead of learning it from a week of silence.

The platforms are now saying this out loud. On October 13, Social Media Today reported that Pinterest is asking brands to send people "back into their life" with what they came for, rather than design content that keeps them scrolling. Coming from a company that sells ads, that is a strong hint about what earns attention in 2026.

What is Pinterest telling brands about engagement?

Pinterest's new playbook, which it calls "Design the Exit," starts from social media fatigue among younger users. According to figures Pinterest shared, 40% of Gen Z users wish social media had never been invented, and 63% of Gen Z adults want to unplug in a more intentional way. Pinterest also says searches for "things to do instead of scrolling" rose 365% year over year on its platform.

Its argument is that people in "action mode," looking for something specific, are more likely to buy, trust and remember a brand than people stuck in a scroll loop, where ads simply blend into the feed. The creative advice follows from that: show how a product fits into everyday life, help people see a path to something they want, and once they have found it, let them go.

You don't have to sell on Pinterest for this to apply. A post that solves a small problem gets saved and shared. A post that only asks for attention gets scrolled past.

Why does this keep happening to small brands?

Economists have a name for the root cause: information asymmetry. It describes a situation where one side knows much more than the other. The textbook example is a used-car seller who knows the car's faults while the buyer does not.

On social media the gap runs the other way. Your audience knows exactly what it finds useful, funny or worth saving. You only see the result afterward, as a like count with no explanation. Large brands close that gap with research teams and testing budgets. A small business usually posts on instinct and reads the silence as bad luck.

Without real audience insight, most brands copy whatever seems to work. ToucHigh's competitive-content research found that about 87% of social content crowds the same "authentic" formula. When most posts follow one playbook, sounding real stops being a way to stand out. ToucHigh's team has studied this kind of content homogenization in peer-reviewed research presented at ACM SIGIR.

That is the insight behind this article: true connection thrives where data fails to reach. Likes tell you what happened. They don't tell you what your customer was hoping to find. To learn that, you have to go where the dashboard doesn't: customers' questions, complaints and own words.

How do I make my posts more engaging?

Start with the audience data you already have for free, then build posts from it. This weekly routine takes about an hour.

  1. Mine your inbox for questions. Spend 15 minutes reading recent DMs, reviews, support emails and comments. Write down every question a customer asked, in their words.
  2. Turn the top three questions into posts. One question, one post. "Will this fit on a 24-inch shelf?" is a better post topic than "Our new collection is here."
  3. Lead with the problem, not the brand. Your first line, or the first second of a video, should name the situation the viewer is in. Save the logo for the end.
  4. Show it working. A 10-second demo, a before-and-after or a quick how-to often does more than a polished product shot, because it answers "what does this do for me?"
  5. Give one clear action. Save this for later, comment with your size, or tap through to the full guide. One action per post, matched to what the post is for.
  6. Reply with substance. Answer comments with real information, not just a heart emoji. Every reply is another chance to learn what people want.

How can I predict engagement before I publish?

You can't know for sure how a post will do. But you can catch most weak posts before they go out by scoring each draft against a few questions. Give each row a 0, 1 or 2, and rewrite anything that scores under 8 out of 12. The example drafts below are illustrations, not real campaigns.

What should I measure after a post goes live?

Likes are the weakest signal. They cost nothing to give and say little about intent. If Pinterest is right that action beats attention, measure the signs of action instead:

  • Saves: the viewer expects to need this again.
  • Shares and sends: the viewer thinks someone else needs it.
  • Comments with questions: a buying signal, and your next post topic.
  • Profile visits and link clicks: the post moved someone one step closer to buying.

Once a month, list your five best posts by saves and shares, and look for what they have in common: topic, format, hook. Make more of that, and score new drafts against it before they go out. Over a few months, your own results become the audience data you were missing.

The insight
True connection thrives where data fails to reach.

Information asymmetry means your audience knows what it values while you only see likes after the fact. Closing that gap with customer questions and pre-publish checks is what turns ignored posts into useful ones.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good engagement rate for a small business?

There is no universal benchmark worth chasing, because engagement rates vary by platform, industry, audience size and how each tool calculates them. The most useful benchmark is your own. Track your average engagement rate per post for a month, then aim to beat it. Comparing saves and shares across your own posts tells you more than comparing likes with a big brand.

Should I post more often to get more engagement?

Not if quality drops. Posting more gives you more chances to be seen, but a stream of forgettable posts teaches your audience to scroll past you. For most small teams, a few posts a week built from real customer questions will earn more saves, shares and replies than a daily post written in a hurry. Raise frequency only when you can keep the quality.

Can AI predict how a social media post will perform?

It can estimate, not promise. Prediction tools compare a draft against patterns in large sets of past content, such as which hooks, formats and topics tended to earn responses, and return a score. That helps you pick stronger drafts before publishing. Audiences change and context matters, so treat any score as a pre-launch filter and confirm with real results after posting.

How do I find out what my audience wants if I have few followers?

Use the conversations you already have. Read reviews of your products and your competitors' products, note every question customers ask by email, phone or in person, and check what people ask in the comments of bigger accounts in your niche. Those questions, in customers' own words, are the best starting list for posts that get saved and shared.
