B2B content marketing that doesn't send decision-makers to sleep

The Stagwell–ServiceNow deal shows B2B budgets are big. Why dull content costs you dearly, and how to create content buyers feel is worth their time.

“How do I make B2B content that genuinely engages and still generates qualified leads?”

How do you create B2B content marketing that genuinely engages and still generates qualified leads? By swapping what the brand wants to say for what the buyer feels they gain. B2B content gets dull when it talks about features. It gets interesting when it names the risk, the pressure and the doubt of the person making the decision, and proves it understands them.

If anyone still thinks B2B is the land of grey PowerPoint, take a look at this week's news. According to Adweek, Stagwell has signed a three-year partnership with ServiceNow, the largest multi-agency deal in the group's history, with Code and Theory, 72andSunny, Allison Worldwide and Movers+Shakers as lead agencies. According to COMvergence, cited by Adweek, ServiceNow spends $120m on media.

What does the Stagwell–ServiceNow deal say about B2B?

Three things. First, that B2B budgets are big and hotly contested. An enterprise AI platform bringing in agencies with strong creative track records is no accident: it's an acknowledgement that selling to businesses takes ideas too.

Second, that contracts and technology now go hand in hand. As part of the deal, Stagwell will adopt ServiceNow's AI platform across its entire organisation. The client isn't just buying campaigns; it's buying an agency that uses what it sells.

Third, that the B2B buyer is a human being. Someone who scrolls their feed between meetings, gets bored and shares whatever makes them look clever in front of the boss. Content that forgets this loses out to any meme.

Why does dull B2B content cost so much?

Value perception theory explains it. Perceived value is the sum the customer does between what they receive and what they give up to receive it: money, but also time, effort and risk. The person doing the sum defines the value, not the person selling.

In B2B content, the price is attention. A 30-page e-book that repeats the website charges a lot and delivers little. A post about "innovative, integrated solutions" charges three seconds and delivers nothing at all. The buyer does that sum without even noticing and keeps scrolling.

Hence an idea many brands find uncomfortable: value is not what you say it is; it's what they feel it is. Engaging B2B content lowers the perceived cost, because it's quick, clear and useful, and raises the perceived gain: a fresh idea, an argument to take to the committee, one less risk to worry about.

What do B2B buyers really value?

Rarely the feature. Almost always the tension behind the purchase:

  • The risk of getting it wrong: nobody wants to be the person who picked the supplier that caused problems.
  • Internal politics: whoever is buying has to convince finance, IT and the board.
  • Time: implementation, the learning curve, months of transition.
  • Reputation: the right purchase is also a career move.

Imagine an agency working for a company that makes management software for distributors. Its current content talks about modules and integrations. A close reading of conversations in the sector might reveal something else entirely: managers afraid of bringing operations to a halt during the system switch. Content that names that fear, and shows how the transition happens without a single lorry standing idle, is worth more than ten posts about features.

How to create B2B content that engages, step by step

  1. List the tensions from real conversations. LinkedIn comments, industry forums, product reviews, questions that reach the sales team. Don't invent the pain point; find it.
  2. Read what competitors publish and advertise. Note where everyone says the same thing. There's no room for you there.
  3. Write several concepts, one per tension. Five to ten short routes, each with a headline and a clear promise.
  4. Test before you invest. Score the concepts and put media behind only those most likely to work.
  5. Produce in the format the buyer actually consumes. Carousels for the feed, short video for the commute, in-depth material for those already evaluating suppliers.
  6. Measure qualified leads, not vanity clicks. Agree with sales what a good lead looks like and close the loop every month.

How do you know a B2B creative will work before you spend?

This is where many agencies are still working in the dark. The B2B sales cycle is long, the media budget is finite and the learning arrives late: by the time the report shows the ad didn't work, the quarter is over.

No score replaces the eye of someone who knows the client. But it shifts the conversation from "I think this one's better" to "this one has a better chance, and here's why". Which, let's face it, is a far less boring conversation.

Frequently asked questions

Does B2B content need to be serious?

It needs to be relevant, not po-faced. The B2B buyer is the same person who consumes personal content for the rest of the day and compares your brand with everything they've seen in their feed. Humour, storytelling and opinion work in B2B when they serve a real buyer tension. What doesn't work is a generic tone about innovative solutions.

What's the difference between engagement and a qualified lead in B2B?

Engagement measures attention: clicks, comments, shares. A qualified lead measures purchase intent within the profile the sales team can convert. The two are related but not the same: a viral post can attract the wrong audience. Agree qualification criteria with sales and track lead-to-opportunity conversion, not just volume.

How do I find B2B buyers' real pain points?

Start with what's already public: LinkedIn comments and discussions, industry forums and communities, product reviews in the category and competitors' content. Add the questions that reach the sales and support teams. Group everything by theme and frequency. A pain point that shows up across several sources, in the buyer's own words, is raw material for content.

Is it worth pre-testing creative in B2B campaigns?

Especially in B2B, where the sales cycle is long and media learning arrives slowly. Scoring concepts before investing helps concentrate budget on the most promising routes and justify choices to the client with data. Pre-testing doesn't replace creative judgement, but it reduces the cost of discovering too late that a piece wasn't working.
