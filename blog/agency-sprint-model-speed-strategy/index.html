The agency sprint model: how to move fast without breaking strategy

Agencies are selling five-day sprints instead of retainers. Here's how to move that fast without the strategy falling apart, and which chores to hand to AI agents.

The question behind this article

“How can my agency adapt strategy quickly enough to keep up with digital advertising, without the work getting sloppy?”

Short answerWork in short, fixed sprints, but keep strategy outside the sprint. Decide the few things that must not change for a quarter, run tactics in sprints of days or weeks, and automate the monitoring, reporting and competitor checks that usually get skipped when time is tight. Speed comes from removing chores, not from removing rigour.

Key takeaways

  • The agency sprint model sells fixed, short engagements measured in days or weeks instead of retainers or months-long projects.
  • Campaign US reports that X&O's sprints average three to four weeks and that clients return several times a year instead of signing agency-of-record contracts.
  • The main risk of sprints is unconnected work: sharp tactics with no strategy holding them together.
  • A one-page strategy spine that lasts a quarter lets sprints move quickly on tactics without rewriting the strategy each time.
  • Automating monitoring, reporting and resizing protects rigour, because the checks that usually get skipped are produced without anyone having to find the time.

How do you adapt strategy fast enough to keep up with digital advertising without the work getting sloppy? Work in short sprints, but keep the strategy outside them. Tactics should move in days or weeks. The audience, the core tension and the definition of success should move deliberately, and the checks that keep work honest should be automated so they never get skipped.

Plenty of agencies are already selling speed as the product. Campaign US calls it "a constant hustle": agencies selling fixed sprints measured in days or weeks instead of months-long projects or agency-of-record retainers. Beers With Friends works in five-day sprints. X&O runs sprints from four days to three months, averaging three to four weeks, and says it has "zero client retention" because clients come back several times a year rather than signing AOR contracts. It has even recast AOR as "Answers On Repeat".

What is the agency sprint model?

A sprint is a fixed, short engagement focused on one client problem and one set of outputs. According to Campaign US, the agencies using it share a few habits: one client and one project at a time, clients heavily involved throughout, and repeat hiring instead of a retainer. Gus, another agency in the piece, uses sprints selectively and sizes each one to the request and its deliverables.

The appeal is obvious. Clients get answers in the time it takes a traditional agency to schedule the kick-off. The agency gets focus and a clear finish line. Campaign notes that the model changes how agencies think about pricing, utilisation and retention.

It is not easy. X&O's Brett Banker calls it a "big bet operationally", and his co-founder Eric Segal says the pace "keeps us honest, hungry and it forces us to continually deliver."

Why does speed so often cost strategy?

Because under time pressure, teams cut the slow, invisible work first. Picture a five-day sprint run badly. Monday is the brief. Tuesday to Thursday is making. Friday is the presentation. The competitor check was squeezed into a Monday-morning skim. The audience read was whatever the team remembered from the last project. Nobody wrote down how success would be measured.

The output looks fine. Nobody can say why it should work. The next sprint starts from scratch, and the one after that quietly contradicts the first.

That is the real risk of speed. Not bad work, but unconnected work: a string of sharp tactics with no strategy holding them together.

Why it keeps happening: cultural lag

Social change theory has a useful idea for this. The sociologist William Ogburn called it cultural lag: technology changes quickly, the norms and institutions around it change slowly, and the gap between them causes strain.

Agencies live in that gap. Platforms, formats and AI tools change monthly. Approval chains, timesheets, scopes of work and reporting rituals change yearly, if that. Teams bridge the gap with heroics: late nights, skipped steps, the research that will be done "next time".

The insight we keep coming back to is simple: in the pursuit of speed, let not integrity be the casualty. Slowing down is not the answer. Rebuilding the rigour so it runs at the new speed is.

How do I run sprints without breaking strategy?

  1. Write a strategy spine that lasts a quarter. One page: the audience, the core tension, the positioning and what success means. Sprints can challenge the spine, but they should not silently rewrite it.
  2. Start every sprint with fresh evidence on day one. Competitor activity, audience conversation and last sprint's results should be waiting when the sprint opens, not assembled by a junior on day three.
  3. Keep the room small. Banker told Campaign US that too many stakeholders who need to buy into the direction "slow us down". Agree upfront who decides, and keep everyone else informed rather than involved.
  4. Ship every output with a reason. One line per deliverable: which part of the spine it serves and what evidence supports it.
  5. End with measurement, not just a deck. Decide what you will check, when, and what result would make you stop.
  6. Keep a learning log. If clients come back several times a year, "zero retention" must not mean zero memory. Record what each sprint found so the next one starts ahead.

What should I speed up, and what should I protect?

Not all work deserves the same pace. Split it deliberately.

WorkMake it fastKeep it human
Competitor monitoringAlways-on, automated trackingDeciding which moves matter
Audience researchLive conversation feedsChoosing the tension to build on
Performance reportingAutomated assembly of the numbersExplaining why results moved
Asset productionAutomated resizing and versioningThe idea and the craft
Status updatesShort written updatesThe conversation about direction

Is the sprint model right for my agency?

It suits problems with a clear question and a clear end: a launch idea, a positioning refresh, a campaign concept, a fast response to a competitor's move. It suits agencies with senior people who can make decisions quickly, because sprints leave little room for layers of review.

It suits always-on work less well. Media management, CRM programmes and community management depend on continuity. Many agencies will end up with a hybrid: retainers for the always-on base, sprints for the questions that cannot wait.

Either way, the rule holds. Move fast on tactics. Move deliberately on strategy. Automate the chores that tempt you to skip the checks.

In the pursuit of speed, let not integrity be the casualty.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sprint in an agency context?

A sprint is a fixed, short engagement, typically days or weeks, focused on one client problem and one set of outputs. Campaign US reported examples ranging from Beers With Friends' five-day sprints to X&O's engagements, which average three to four weeks. Clients are heavily involved and often hire the agency repeatedly rather than signing an agency-of-record retainer.

How do you keep strategic consistency across sprints?

Separate the strategy from the sprint. Agree a strategy spine covering audience, core tension, positioning and success measures that holds for a quarter, and let sprints test tactics against it. Keep a shared learning log so each sprint starts from what the last one found, and review the spine deliberately rather than letting it drift.

Does the sprint model hurt agency retention?

It changes what retention means. X&O told Campaign US it has "zero client retention" because clients come back several times a year instead of signing AOR contracts. Revenue becomes less predictable, so the agency has to earn every return visit through results and through the client's experience of working with it.

What should be automated in a sprint?

Anything repetitive that needs no judgement: assembling performance reports, monitoring competitors and audience conversation, resizing assets and compiling status updates. Keep people on choosing the insight, shaping the idea and interpreting results. The aim is to give the sprint back the days currently lost to chores, not to shrink the thinking.
